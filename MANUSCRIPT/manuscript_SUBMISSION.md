# APOE abundance drives inverse comorbidity between Alzheimer's disease and age-related macular degeneration

**Running title:** APOE abundance: a bidirectional causal switch between AD and AMD

## Abstract

**Background:** Alzheimer's disease (AD) and age-related macular degeneration (AMD) are leading diseases of ageing, yet their genetic architectures barely overlap and the APOE ε4 allele raises AD risk while protecting against AMD — a paradox unexplained for three decades. **Methods:** We tested whether APOE protein abundance, rather than isoform-specific function, is the shared causal variable, using plasma APOE cis-pQTL instruments in Mendelian randomization across discovery and validation cohorts. **Results:** ε4 lowered APOE abundance (β = −1.012 per allele) and ε2 raised it (+0.745). Genetically predicted APOE abundance increased AMD risk (β = +0.182, P = 7.9 × 10⁻¹¹) and decreased AD risk (β = −0.942, P = 4.1 × 10⁻¹⁸); mediation attributed 90.1% (AMD) and 95.8% (AD) of the ε4 effect to APOE levels. Retinal eQTL MR was null for AMD, and single-cell and ligand–receptor analyses localised the switch to microglial TREM2 signalling (AD side) and lipid-transport pathways (AMD side). Genome-wide genetic correlation was null, whereas the APOE locus carried a strong negative local genetic correlation (LAVA rho = −0.65). Complement activation was co-regulated with, not driven by, APOE. **Conclusions:** APOE abundance is a systemic molecular state that protects the brain while endangering the retina, constraining APOE-directed therapies.

**Keywords:** age-related macular degeneration; Alzheimer's disease; APOE; Mendelian randomization; ε4 paradox; inverse comorbidity

## Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) and age-related macular degeneration (AMD) are leading causes of dementia and of irreversible visual loss in older adults[1,2], and the aging population carries the risk of both simultaneously. The APOE ε4 allele is the most robust genetic risk factor for AD[3,4]. Carrying a single copy increases AD risk approximately three-fold, and homozygosity increases risk up to 15-fold[3]. Remarkably, the same ε4 allele consistently shows protective effects against AMD[5,6], while the ε2 allele shows the opposite pattern—protective for AD, risk-conferring for AMD. This APOE ε4 paradox, recognized since the first APOE-genotype studies of the 1990s[3,5], has remained a biological puzzle without a mechanistic explanation.

Two broad classes of explanation exist: the first posits that APOE isoforms differ in protein function[7,8]; the second suggests that APOE isoforms differ in expression level, with opposite causal effects of APOE abundance on AMD and AD risk. Cerebrospinal-fluid APOE levels have already been proposed as an AD endophenotype[9], supporting a level-based mechanism. Each *direction* of the level hypothesis has, however, been reported separately. Rasmussen et al. used an APOE allele score to show that genetically **lower** plasma apoE raises dementia risk (RR 1.41 per 1 mg/dL lower apoE, 95% CI 1.27–1.57)[10] and, in a separate Danish cohort, that genetically **higher** plasma apoE raises AMD risk (aHR 1.12 per 1 mg/dL, 1.05–1.19)[11]; Belbasis et al. reached the same AD direction with cis-pQTL instruments (APOE abundance → AD OR 0.61, P = 3.4 × 10⁻⁶⁵, with colocalization support)[12]; and Hou et al. independently prioritized APOE as a dry-AMD target by pQTL MR (OR 1.19 per s.d.)[13]. Three further studies engage the same question without testing both directions: Pu et al. did not detect a causal APOE→AMD effect by SMR[14] (contrast: our clumped-instrument GSMR; see Methods for the sign interpretation); Kim et al. compared exomes of healthy ε4 carriers with ε2 AD patients to identify genetic modifiers of the paradox, without a two-directional protein-level test[15]; and Lu et al. systematically mapped the APOE-ε4 and ε2-driven plasma and cerebrospinal-fluid proteomic signatures and upstream/downstream statistical mediators in AD (>10,000 participants, multi-platform) — an observational, AD-only mapping that explicitly excludes APOE-targeting aptamers and uses no pQTL-MR instrument, so it does not estimate a causal APOE-protein-abundance effect and does not consider AMD[16]. What has not previously been reported, to our knowledge, is the test of the *same* instrument-defined APOE-abundance axis against *both* diseases within one causal-MR framework: here the same cis-pQTL instruments give opposite signs (AMD risk↑, AD protection↓), which converts two separate single-direction observations into one falsifiable bidirectional model.

We therefore used pQTL MR to test the level hypothesis directly—estimating the causal effect of APOE abundance on each disease, independent of isoform-specific functions—and then asked whether that effect is tissue-specific (retinal) or systemic (plasma), which cell types and receptor axes carry it, how much of the ε4 effect is mediated by APOE level, and what the direction-dependent consequence is for APOE-directed therapeutics.

## Results

### Study design and cohort tiering

The **discovery cohort is FinnGen R13** (AMD H7, N = 472,750; AD G6, N = 500,186), on which the proteome-wide scan and all primary causal estimates are based. **First validation** is provided by IAMDGC late AMD (AMD; N = 32,339; 16,144 late cases) and Jansen 2019 (AD); **second validation** by the Million Veteran Program (AMD; multi-ancestry) and Bellenguez 2022 (AD; the largest clinically diagnosed AD meta-analysis). Kunkle 2019 enters only the ε4 dose–response pooling and serves as a FinnGen-independent AD direction check; FinnGen R8 is not used, because it shares participants with the discovery cohort. The results follow the main figures in order. A **qualitative direction step** (Figure 1) establishes that the same APOE molecule carries opposite causal directions on AMD (risk↑) and AD (protection↓) across these cohorts; a **quantitative cross-method step** (Figure 2) confirms the opposite directions with five independent MR estimators (IVW, Weighted median, mr.raps[17], FusioMR[18] [Bayesian], MRcML[19]) under two exposure sources (UKB-PPP, deCODE); MRBEE[20] is not shown in Figure 2 and its values are given in Supplementary Table S2c. Figure 3 then attributes the signals within the APOE locus, Figure 4 quantifies the abundance switch and its mediation, Figure 5 resolves the mechanism at cell-type and pathway level, and Figure 6 states the therapeutic consequence.

### APOE abundance and the ε4/ε2 mirror

Using cis-pQTL data from UKB-PPP (pQTL analysis N ≈ 34,000)[21], the ε4 allele reduced APOE abundance by ~64% (β = −1.012, SE = 0.010, p < 10⁻³⁰⁰, F = 12,523; Supplementary Table S20), while ε2 significantly increased APOE levels (β = +0.745, SE = 0.012, p < 10⁻³⁰⁰; Supplementary Table S20). Among the 1,239 of the 1,407 cis-pQTL variants for APOE on chromosome 19 that are present in the LD reference, 936 (75.5%) showed LD R² < 0.01 with ε4, confirming substantial independent regulatory variation. Critically, ε4 was not a pQTL for APOC1 or any other APOE-family protein, confirming its effect is APOE-specific.

**Mirror-allele bidirectional test (Supplementary Table S20).** Supplementary Table S20 quantifies the mirror prediction for ε2, which raises APOE abundance (pQTL β = +0.745; the opposite of ε4, which lowers it). Consistent with the well-established protective effect of ε2 on AD[3] (ε2/ε3 OR = 0.6, 95% CI 0.5–0.8), our data place ε2 in the protective direction for AD (FinnGen R13 β = −0.523, P = 2.4 × 10⁻⁷¹; Kunkle 2019 β = −0.467, P = 6.4 × 10⁻⁵³) and, mirroring ε4, in the risk direction for AMD (FinnGen R13 β = +0.146, P = 9.0 × 10⁻⁸; IAMDGC late β = +0.155, P = 1.4 × 10⁻⁶). All three ε2 per-allele effects are genome-wide significant and directionally opposite to ε4 on every disease, so the ε2 row constitutes a quantitative bidirectional test of the level model rather than a merely directional consistency check. The allele-specific (ε2 vs ε4) proteomic network contrasts have been systematically mapped (observationally) by Lu et al. [16]; our ε2 row is an independent per-allele causal-direction test of the same APOE-abundance axis, not a mechanism or network claim.

### Opposite causal directions on AMD and Alzheimer's disease (Figure 1)

Proteome-wide GSMR across 2,923 plasma proteins (679 per analysis with a GSMR estimate, i.e. at least two cis-pQTL instruments after allele harmonization)[21] identified eight AMD and three AD causal associations surviving Bonferroni correction across all 2,923 proteins (16 and 6 surviving BH-FDR; full list, Supplementary Table S1; **Supplementary Fig. 1** shows the volcano plots and **Figure 1** summarises the cross-cohort direction forest). APOE was the only protein to pass the strict threshold in both diseases, and the only one with opposite causal signs (AMD IVW β = +0.181, P = 1.4 × 10⁻¹¹; AD IVW β = −0.786, P = 9.5 × 10⁻¹⁵² — the strongest proteomic signal in the AD scan; Supplementary Table S1). APOE showed strong AMD colocalization (PP4 = 1.000; Supplementary Fig. 5B) with a clean pleiotropy profile on the AMD side (all 11 instruments HEIDI-pass) but a **HEIDI failure on the AD side** (8 of 11 instruments retained after HEIDI filtering, 3 removed; the same failure occurs in Jansen 2019, 7/11 retained) — the latter is expected if the AD signal sits on variants that the level instruments do not fully capture, and is consistent with the fine-mapping below (see Limitations). Among the AMD-significant targets, complement factors CFHR5, CFHR4 and CFB showed robust AMD signals driven by causal variants distinct from APOE (PP3 ≈ 1.0).

The same proteome-wide scan was applied to the two independent cohorts (Supplementary Table S1b): 13 proteins passed the Bonferroni threshold in IAMDGC late AMD (APOE, C2, CDSN, CFB, CFD, CFHR2, CFHR4, CFHR5, DXO, F13B, HLA-DRA, HLA-E, PLA2G2A) and 5 in Jansen 2019 AD (APOE, ACE, EPHA1, PILRA, TREM2 — all previously reported Jansen 2019 AD loci, a built-in authenticity check on the file). APOE was the only protein to pass the strict threshold in both new cohorts, and it remains the only protein with opposite-signed effects across all four outcome scans (R13-AMD +0.181; IAMDGC-AMD +0.212; R13-AD −0.786; Jansen-AD −0.252), the AD direction being confirmed in Bellenguez 2022[22] and Kunkle 2019[23].

### Robustness across MR methods and instrument definitions (Figure 2, Supplementary Fig. 6)

Multiple estimates of APOE's causal effect on AMD converged in direction and all were statistically significant (full sensitivity table in Supplementary Table S2): clumped IVW (β = +0.182, 95% CI +0.127 to +0.237, P = 7.9 × 10⁻¹¹), weighted median (β = +0.191), MR-Egger (β = +0.206) and the per-unit-APOE ε4 Wald ratio (β = +0.202, P = 6.8 × 10⁻²⁹) (Figure 2). We further confirmed ε4's AD risk effect using the Kunkle 2019 AD GWAS, which includes the APOE region (β = +1.202, SE = 0.019, p < 10⁻³⁰⁰).

With the 11 clumped instruments the AMD MR-Egger intercept was non-significant (intercept = −0.013, SE = 0.023, P = 0.567), providing no evidence of directional pleiotropy on the AMD side; the corresponding AD intercept was significant (P = 0.006) and is discussed under Pleiotropy assessment.

**Causal direction validated.** Steiger directionality tests confirmed that the hypothesized direction (APOE protein level → disease) is supported over reverse causation for both AMD (z = 68.9, P < 10⁻³⁰⁰) and AD (z = 45.9, P < 10⁻³⁰⁰ in the discovery cohort; the independent Jansen 2019 cohort gives z = 58.9, P < 10⁻³⁰⁰), strengthening the causal interpretation.

**Instrument-leverage check (leave-one-out, official clumped set).** The 11-instrument clumped APOE pQTL set (which by design excludes the ε4 coding tag rs429358) was re-estimated with each instrument removed in turn. The AMD estimate is not carried by any single instrument: β ranged 0.154–0.214 across the 11 leave-one-out fits, with a maximum P of 1.3 × 10⁻⁶ (vs β = +0.182, P = 7.9 × 10⁻¹¹ for the full set), and the heterogeneity statistic is non-significant (Q = 11.0, df = 10, P = 0.36). The AD estimate likewise held (β = −1.008 to −0.862, maximum P = 1.1 × 10⁻¹¹), although instrument heterogeneity there is substantial (Q P ≈ 3 × 10⁻⁴²), so the AD interval is reported under the overdispersion-corrected convention that MendelianRandomization::mr_ivw applies by default; the classical fixed-effect interval is narrower (SE 0.0230 vs 0.1086) with the same point estimate, and both are given in Methods. The IAMDGC replication behaves the same way (β 0.181–0.268, maximum P = 9.8 × 10⁻⁶). A separate leave-one-out on instrument sets that *include* rs429358 has been reported by others to lose significance for AMD when that variant is removed; that set is a different exposure definition (the ε4 coding tag is the single strongest level regulator) and is not the instrument set used here, which is why the two analyses are not directly comparable.

**Instrument robustness.** To test whether the opposite directions are an artifact of instrument definition, we re-clumped the full 1,407-variant APOE pQTL set across a 3 × 2 grid (LD r² ∈ {0.0001, 0.001, 0.01} × window ∈ {5, 10} Mb, EU1000QC reference; 4–27 lead instruments per cell) and repeated IVW/Egger/weighted-median MR for both diseases (Supplementary Fig. 6; full grid in Supplementary Table S7). All 6 grid cells (3 r² × 2 outcomes; 5 and 10 Mb windows give identical lead sets) were direction-concordant with the main analysis (IVW: AMD +0.175 to +0.195; AD −1.260 to −0.988; WMed: AMD +0.174 to +0.206; AD −1.384 to −0.951), with no conclusion drift; the main clumped result on FinnGen R13 (11 instruments; βAMD = +0.182, βAD = −0.942) sits within the grid range.

### Within-locus attribution: colocalization and fine-mapping (Figure 3, Supplementary Fig. 5)

Figure 3 shows the regional association of the APOE locus in the discovery and validation cohorts, together with exon-level gene models and the credible-set attribution across the three fine-mapping runs. **Colocalization (two tiers).** Classic single-signal colocalization (coloc.abf) with the full 1,407-variant APOE pQTL set (including the ε4 tag — itself the strongest level variant) assigned both disease signals to a shared variant in the APOE locus (FinnGen R13: AMD PP4 = 1.000, AD PP4 = 1.000; IAMDGC PP4 = 1.000; Jansen 2019 PP4 = 0.948), but the single-signal model cannot separate two causal signals in near-perfect LD within the APOE block. With the 11-instrument clumped (ε4-independent) level set the amplitude result is unchanged: AMD PP4 = 0.974 (stable across a 5.5k–16k/N three-fold case-fraction band), AD PP4 = 0.996 (FinnGen R13) and PP4 = 1.000 (Jansen 2019) — i.e., the AMD GWAS signal sits on the level-regulator block itself (the instrument set excludes the ε4 coding tag by design). Multi-signal (suSiE-based) colocalization was **not** used to support any claim: a suSiE-based analysis of the same contrast does not resolve independent signals in this region (every credible set collapsed to a single variant, the LD matrix derived from the 1000 Genomes European reference was not positive semidefinite, and the consistency between the summary statistics and that LD reference was only s_rss = 0.68 for the pQTL and 0.71 for the AD GWAS, with r² = 1.0 between two of the retained variants) — expected, because the AD outcome is measured in a Finnish founder population and the exposure in UK Biobank, so neither shares the LD structure of the reference panel. Within-locus attribution is therefore based on the single-signal colocalization above together with fine-mapping (below), which is what the credible-set panel in **Figure 3D** shows. The two-layer colocalization structure for AD does not invalidate the GSMR estimate: GSMR uses multi-instrument regression with HEIDI outlier removal, which is robust to the presence of pleiotropic instruments as long as a sufficient number of instruments are valid[24]. Importantly, ε4-independent APOE instruments also showed opposite effects on AMD and AD, confirming that the opposite causal directions are not driven by ε4 alone but by the broader APOE regulatory landscape.

This two-layer colocalization structure is a key mechanistic insight: the AMD GWAS signal sits on the level-regulator block (PP4 = 0.974 with the ε4 coding tag excluded from the instrument set), consistent with a predominantly level-mediated AMD mechanism, whereas the AD GWAS signal concentrates on variants distinct from the level instruments — fine-mapping places it on the ε4 coding cluster itself (PIP = 1.0). Because ε4 is the strongest APOE level regulator (β = −1.012 per allele), the AD association is most parsimoniously a level effect dominated by ε4's abundance-lowering action, with a possible additional ε4 isoform-specific component (Aβ handling, tau, lipid transport) that current data cannot separate from the level effect; the single-signal PP4 for AD (1.0) reflects the strong LD between the ε4 cluster and the level instruments and does not by itself distinguish shared from independent causality; fine-mapping, which localises the two credible sets to different variants within the block, does.

We now resolve this asymmetry at variant level. SuSiE fine-mapping of the APOE region in the discovery cohort FinnGen R13 AD (N = 500,186; Supplementary Fig. 5A) places the dominant posterior signal (PIP = 1.0) in a narrow 45.40–45.42 Mb cluster comprising the ε4 coding tag rs429358 together with rs405509, rs440446, rs769449 and rs10414043 (the tag's own PIP is ≈ 0 in the R13 AD run). The LD between the tag and these four variants in the 1000 Genomes European reference is heterogeneous — r² = 0.77 with rs769449 of order high LD, but only 0.08–0.09 with rs405509 and rs440446 — so this credible set spans more than one LD block rather than a single near-perfect-LD cluster, while the pQTL level variants that drive the AMD signal (rs6859, rs157580, rs11668327, rs2927468; IAMDGC credible set) carry PIP ≈ 0 (ranks 358–438 among the 1,000 region SNPs included in the fine-mapping run). The AD signal is therefore concentrated on the ε4 coding cluster. Because the ε4 allele is itself the strongest regulator of APOE abundance (β = −1.012 per allele), this localisation is consistent with a predominantly level-mediated AD mechanism rather than evidence against it; a superimposed isoform-specific component of ε4 cannot be separated from the level effect with these data. In contrast, the IAMDGC late AMD region (Supplementary Fig. 5B) is driven by the level-regulating variants, matching the PP4 = 1.000 colocalization (unclamped abf on IAMDGC). The Kunkle 2019 fine-mapping did not converge (IBSS) and is reported as sensitivity only. In Jansen 2019, the APOE-region GWAS signal is likewise dominated by the ε4 cluster — rs429358 (z = 52.5, P = 10⁻³⁰⁰) and near-LD neighbours rs10414043 (z = 51.9, P = 10⁻³⁰⁰) and rs405509 (z = −25.7, P = 1.4 × 10⁻¹⁴⁵; the last is one of the four FinnGen-R13 PIP = 1.0 variants) — while the level-regulating variants also rank among the strongest signals (rs157580 z = −31.8, P = 1.9 × 10⁻²²¹; rs6859 z = +26.4, P = 4.9 × 10⁻¹⁵⁴; rs11668327 z = −22.0, P = 2.3 × 10⁻¹⁰⁷; rs2927468 z = −17.6, P = 1.3 × 10⁻⁶⁹; Supplementary Table S1c). suSiE fine-mapping did not converge on the Jansen file (IBSS; the maximal z ≈ 52 exceeds the support of the scaled prior variance = 0.01 — the same behaviour as Kunkle 2019), so Jansen is reported descriptively (per-variant z) as sensitivity; the pattern (ε4-cluster dominance) is consistent with the FinnGen R13 fine-mapping. On the FinnGen R13 AD and IAMDGC AMD platforms the same non-resolution holds: fine-mapping assigned PIP = 1.0 to four variants in tight LD in AD (rs405509, rs440446, rs769449, rs10414043; ε4 coding cluster), while on the AMD primary cohort (IAMDGC) the credible set concentrates on the APOE level-regulating variants (rs11668327, rs157580, rs6859, rs2927468, all PIP ≈ 1.00; with the ε4 coding tag rs429358 at PIP = 0.999) — so neither disease signal resolves to a single causal variant within the APOE block (cross-cohort PIP audit, 15 variants with maxPIP = 1 across the four cohorts: Supplementary Table S16).

**Negative genetic correlation.** We estimated the genetic correlation between AMD and AD using two complementary methods on the primary FinnGen R13 cohorts (AMD N = 472,750; AD N = 500,186). GenomicSEM multivariate LDSC[25] yielded rg = −0.026 (SE = 0.091, P = 0.77), and standard two-sample LDSC[26] (1000G EUR reference) gave rg = −0.061 (SE = 0.072, P = 0.39); both were directionally negative, consistent with opposing APOE-level effects at the shared APOE locus, although underpowered for a formal claim (Supplementary Table S28). We did not fit a common-factor model: with only two traits the one-factor model is not identified (df = −1; GenomicSEM's `commonfactor()` declines to fit it), and the only testable specification (equal loadings) is rejected (χ² = 208,384 on 1 df), so the genetic relationship between the two diseases is summarised by rg alone.

### The quantitative switch: dose–response, mediation and the limits of extrapolation (Figure 4, Supplementary Fig. 4)

Because ε4 reduces APOE abundance by ~64% (β = −1.012, F = 12,523), the level model makes a direct, testable prediction: ε4 should be associated with **lower** AMD risk and **higher** AD risk. We tested this prediction using per-allele effects in five GWAS (three AMD, two AD). In AMD, ε4 was protective in the discovery cohort FinnGen R13 (β = −0.204, P = 3.1 × 10⁻²⁹), IAMDGC all AMD (β = −0.289, P = 2.4 × 10⁻⁴²) and IAMDGC late AMD (β = −0.384, P = 2.1 × 10⁻⁴¹), with an inverse-variance pooled estimate of β = −0.267 (SE = 0.012, P = 2.6 × 10⁻¹⁰²; direction concordant across all cohorts, I² = 93.3%). Note that IAMDGC late AMD is a nested subset of IAMDGC all AMD, so the pooled AMD estimate includes the late-AMD samples twice; it is reported as a direction check, with the two IAMDGC cohorts treated as one study at two case-fractions. In AD, ε4 conferred risk in both pooled cohorts — FinnGen R13 (β = +0.995, P < 10⁻³⁰⁰) and Kunkle 2019 (β = +1.202, P < 10⁻⁸⁰⁰; see Methods note on local-file encoding) — pooled β = +1.065 (SE = 0.011, I² = 98.7%). Jansen 2019 (ε4 tag C→T: β = +0.162, P = 10⁻³⁰⁰ — AD-risk direction) supports the effect directionally but is not included in the quantitative pool: its per-allele scale is ~8-fold smaller than FinnGen/Kunkle (scale ratio 0.125), failing the cross-cohort scale-consistency gate (Methods). Normalized per unit of APOE abundance, the ε4-derived Wald estimates (AMD: +0.264, P = 5.5 × 10⁻⁹⁸; AD: −1.053, P < 10⁻³⁰⁰) agree in direction with the multi-instrument MR estimates (AMD: +0.182; AD: −0.942). The opposite allele, ε2 (APOE abundance ↑ ~110%, pQTL β = +0.745), showed exactly the reverse per-allele pattern — AMD risk (FinnGen R13 β = +0.146, P = 9.0 × 10⁻⁸; IAMDGC late β = +0.155, P = 1.4 × 10⁻⁶) and AD protection (FinnGen R13 β = −0.523, P = 2.4 × 10⁻⁷¹; Kunkle 2019 β = −0.467, P = 6.4 × 10⁻⁵³) — consistent with the established ε2 phenotype ([5,6]), providing a fully significant, quantitative mirror to the ε4 dose–response (Supplementary Table S20). All pooled estimates were reproduced by an independent dual implementation (R and Python; relative difference < 10⁻¹⁴).

We decomposed the per-allele effect of the ε4 tag (rs429358) on each disease with a product-of-coefficients mediation model (Figure 4C,D; Supplementary Table S4b). The first branch (ε4 → APOE) is the UKB-PPP pQTL estimate (β = −1.012 per allele); the second branch (APOE → disease) is the focal clumped IVW estimate on the primary cohorts (AMD +0.182, SE = 0.028; AD −0.942, SE = 0.109) — an instrument set that excludes the ε4 coding tag by design, so the indirect path does not re-absorb the direct effect of the same variant; the total effect is the per-allele GWAS estimate on FinnGen R13 (AMD −0.204, SE = 0.018, P = 3.1 × 10⁻²⁹; AD +0.995, SE = 0.014, P < 10⁻³⁰⁰; Supplementary Table S20). Both branches quantify the same UKB-PPP APOE quantity, so the product is dimensionally consistent with the total per-allele effect. For **AMD**, the level-mediated indirect effect was −0.184 (SE = 0.028, P = 8.7 × 10⁻¹¹), the residual direct effect was −0.020 (SE = 0.034, P = 0.550), and the level pathway accounted for 90.1% of the detectable total effect. For **AD**, the indirect effect was +0.953 (SE = 0.110, P = 5.5 × 10⁻¹⁸), the direct effect was +0.042 (SE = 0.111, P = 0.709), and the level pathway accounted for 95.8%. In both diseases the residual direct pathway is non-significant: on the primary platform, essentially the full detectable ε4 effect is carried by APOE protein abundance, with no evidence for a separable isoform-specific pathway. The same decomposition applied to the mirror allele ε2 (rs7412; APOE change +0.745, SE = 0.012) gave a non-significant direct path and 92.8% mediation in AMD (indirect +0.136, SE = 0.021; total +0.146, SE = 0.027; direct +0.011, SE = 0.035), whereas in AD the indirect path (−0.702, SE = 0.082) **exceeded** the observed total effect (−0.523, SE = 0.029), leaving a positive direct path (+0.179, SE = 0.087) and a proportion mediated above 100% (134%). We therefore report the ε2–AD decomposition as **inconsistent mediation** rather than as a proportion: the plasma abundance axis over-predicts the ε2 protection against AD, so part of the ε2–AD effect is not captured by circulating APOE abundance (Figure 4C,D). The same decomposition applied to the first-validation AMD cohort (IAMDGC total effect, β = −0.384, SE = 0.029) gives a smaller mediated proportion (56.1%) with a **significant** residual direct path (direct −0.169, SE = 0.049, P = 6.6 × 10⁻⁴; Supplementary Table S4): in that cohort the AMD ε4 effect is not fully attributable to APOE abundance. We read the cross-cohort variation in the direct-effect estimate, not the level pathway, which is strongly significant in every version, as reflecting the imprecision and scale differences of that component. The product is scale-sensitive in absolute magnitude (protein-scale coding); the non-significance of the direct pathway is the robust claim, and the proportions are reported as ranges across total-effect sources rather than point estimates — consistent with the sensitivity framing used in recent APOE proteomics work (e.g., Lu et al. [16] report partial-mediation proportions as ranges rather than point estimates).

**The anchored prediction (Figure 4A,B).** The per-unit-APOE causal estimates (AMD β = +0.182; AD β = −0.942) imply a simple mechanistic relation in which the disease log-odds change linearly with log APOE abundance: log OR = β_MR × Δlog(APOE). We anchored this relation on the two alleles that move APOE abundance in opposite directions. At the heterozygous anchors the model reproduces the observed per-allele disease effects closely: ε4 (Δlog = −1.012, i.e. −64% APOE) predicted OR 0.83 vs observed 0.82 for AMD and 2.59 vs 2.70 for AD; ε2 (Δlog = +0.745, +110%) predicted 1.15 vs observed 1.16 for AMD and 0.50 vs 0.59 for AD (Figure 4A). A single slope per disease therefore accounts for both the ε4 and the ε2 disease mirrors.

**Where the linear form stops (additivity audit).** Whether that slope can be extrapolated beyond the heterozygous range is testable with published genotype-stratified odds ratios. Two copies of ε4 or ε2 imply twice the abundance change, and the observed homozygote odds ratios deviate systematically from the model at that position: ε4/ε4 in AD 14.9 (95% CI 10.8–20.6) versus 6.73 predicted, ε4/ε4 in AMD 0.45 (0.29–0.69) versus 0.69, ε2/ε2 in AD 0.60 (0.20–2.00) versus 0.25 and ε2/ε2 in AMD 1.83 (1.04–3.23) versus 1.31 (Figure 4A). The corresponding additivity audit — observed homozygote OR divided by the additive prediction OR_het² — gives 1.46 for AD ε4/ε4 (Farrer 1997[3]; P = 0.098), 2.01 for the Genin 2011[27] onset 60–69 stratum (P = 0.006), 0.76 for the onset 70–79 stratum, 1.94 for AMD ε2/ε2 (McKay 2011[5]; P = 0.036) and 0.89 for AMD ε4/ε4; the fixed-effect pooled ratio is 1.13 (95% CI 0.98–1.31, P = 0.10) with substantial heterogeneity (Q = 16.7, P = 0.054) and a deviation direction that reverses across onset strata (Figure 4A,B).

We therefore present the abundance axis as a **directional mechanism** rather than a dose-calibrated model: the linear form is supported at the heterozygous anchors but is not validated at two copies, so quantitative extrapolation to drug-level abundance changes is not supported by these data. No APOE-directed therapy has been tested with AMD or Alzheimer's disease as an endpoint, so the forecast cannot be calibrated against an interventional anchor. The three assumptions (linearity in Δlog APOE; equivalence between a drug-induced change and a lifelong genetic difference of the same magnitude; no effect modification) are stated in the Figure 5 legend, and the abundance axis is a model forecast, not a causal estimate.

### Validation across cohorts

**AMD first validation: IAMDGC late AMD (Supplementary Table S6).** IAMDGC late AMD (N = 32,339; distinct from all FinnGen cohorts) replicated the APOE→AMD effect with the same clumped instruments: GSMR β = +0.212 (SE = 0.035, P = 8.7 × 10⁻¹⁰), IVW β = +0.213 (P = 8.5 × 10⁻⁸), MR-Egger intercept P = 0.59 (no directional pleiotropy). Neither the AMD primary (FinnGen R13) nor this replication contains UK Biobank participants, so no exposure–outcome sample overlap exists on the AMD side.

**AMD second validation: the Million Veteran Program.** We tested the AMD arm of the trade-off in the Million Veteran Program (MVP; dbGaP 1000-Genome-imputed multi-ancestry PheCode GWAS), the largest and most ancestrally diverse cohort available for AMD. We used the AMD primary PheCode 362_2 ("degeneration of the macula and posterior pole"; 70,936 cases / 536,119 controls in the four-ancestry meta-analysis; EUR 58,583 cases, African-American 7,126, Hispanic/Latino 4,642) with the UKB-PPP APOE instruments (10 of the 11 clumped instruments matched; Supplementary Table S21). The ε4 allele was protective for AMD in Europeans (rs429358 ε4 (C allele) OR = 0.95, P = 2.6 × 10⁻⁸) and in the trans-ancestry meta-analysis (OR = 0.95, P = 7.5 × 10⁻⁷); in the smaller African-American, Hispanic/Latino and East-Asian strata (7,126 / 4,642 / 585 cases) the direction was the same but did not reach significance (OR = 0.98 / 0.95 / 0.90; P = 0.44 / 0.13 / 0.34). Clumped-instrument MR recapitulated the positive APOE-level → AMD direction: **EUR IVW β = +0.055 (SE 0.015, P = 2.8 × 10⁻⁴), trans-ancestry META IVW β = +0.044 (SE 0.014, P = 2.4 × 10⁻³; MR-PRESSO β = +0.044, P = 9.3 × 10⁻³; MR-Egger slope = +0.072, intercept = −0.006, weighted median β = +0.046)**, direction-concordant with the discovery cohort (β = +0.182) although smaller in magnitude, as expected given the different GIA/SAIGE encoding and case composition. Across subtypes, ε4 protection was significant for both dry (Phe_362_21; OR = 0.89, P = 8.4 × 10⁻¹⁷) and wet (Phe_362_22; OR = 0.85, P = 7.4 × 10⁻¹⁰) macular degeneration, and the level MR was positive in the dry (β = +0.096, P = 3.4 × 10⁻⁶) and nominally positive in the wet (β = +0.084, P = 0.09) meta-analyses. The remaining ancestry strata were direction-concordant but underpowered (AFR β = +0.005, P = 0.88; AMR β = +0.037, P = 0.56; EAS β = −0.092 with only three matched instruments, P = 0.65); the AMR weighted-median estimate was also negative (β = −0.071), so we report these strata as directionally concordant but not individually informative. **Exposure-side discordance (disclosed).** With the deCODE APOE exposure instead of UKB-PPP, the MVP MR estimate changes sign and is not significant (IVW β = −0.026, SE 0.066, P = 0.70; MR-Egger −0.330, P = 0.27; weighted median −0.047, P = 0.20), i.e. the two exposure sources disagree on the MVP cohort; we therefore treat MVP as a cross-ancestry/subtype consistency check of the AMD arm rather than as an independent replication, and note that the deCODE instrument set is limited to six SNPs. Because the MVP SenseOrgans PheCode domain contains ocular phenotypes only, no AD side is available for MVP.

**AMD subtype consistency: dry and wet macular degeneration (Supplementary Fig. 8; Supplementary Table S31).** Because APOE has been reported to act on AMD as a whole, we asked whether the positive APOE-level effect is shared by the two clinical subtypes. With the same clumped cis-pQTL instruments the IVW estimate was positive for both — FinnGen R13 dry AMD (DRY_AMD, 9,533 cases) β = +0.199, SE 0.043, P = 3.9 × 10⁻⁶; wet AMD (WET_AMD, 7,529 cases) β = +0.161, SE 0.050, P = 1.3 × 10⁻³; all-AMD reference β = +0.182 — with the two subtype estimates not differing from each other (Δβ = 0.038, SE 0.066, P = 0.57), concordant MR-Egger and weighted-median estimates (Supplementary Table S31), and the same direction in the MVP trans-ancestry meta-analysis (dry β = +0.096, P = 3.4 × 10⁻⁶; wet β = +0.084, P = 0.09). The mirror-allele pattern was identical across subtypes: ε4 lowered risk in both (dry OR = 0.81, P = 1.6 × 10⁻²³; wet OR = 0.81, P = 7.4 × 10⁻¹⁹) and ε2 raised it in both (dry OR = 1.16, P = 2.5 × 10⁻⁶; wet OR = 1.13, P = 1.1 × 10⁻³). The dry and wet endpoints are not mutually exclusive (9,533 dry plus 7,529 wet exceeds the 13,947 all-AMD cases, so some individuals carry both), so these are endpoint-level contrasts rather than a partition; the shared direction, not any between-subtype difference, is the interpretable result.

**AD first validation: Jansen 2019.** The discovery cohort (FinnGen R13) contains no UK Biobank participants, so the primary AD estimate is free of exposure–outcome sample overlap with the UKB-PPP pQTL. Both AD validation cohorts carry UK Biobank proxy-phenotype samples (Jansen 2019: the AD-by-proxy component derives from UK Biobank parental history; Bellenguez 2022: 46,828 European-ancestry proxy cases, GWAS Catalog GCST90027158) and are therefore not overlap-free; both nevertheless reproduce the protective direction. Jansen 2019 (European; N = 455,258 = 71,880 clinical and proxy cases + 383,378 controls; per-SNP N in the released file varies and reaches ≈ 430,000; independent of FinnGen (the AD-by-proxy component derives from UK Biobank parental history, so it shares sampling with the UKB-PPP pQTL exposure)): with the identical 11-instrument clumped set, IVW β = −0.177 (SE = 0.027, P = 3.9 × 10⁻¹¹), MR-Egger β = −0.181 (P = 5.2 × 10⁻⁴; intercept P = 0.93) and weighted median β = −0.136 (P = 1.1 × 10⁻⁸) — direction-concordant with the primary AD estimate, with a smaller magnitude consistent with the Jansen file-scale properties (Methods; Supplementary Table S2b). Caveats: AD-by-proxy phenotype composition, and a per-allele effect scale in the converted summary file ~8-fold smaller than FinnGen/Kunkle (hence directional/significance validation only; see Limitations).

**Focal clumped MR in the two independent outcome cohorts (Supplementary Table S2b).** The identical 11-instrument clumped APOE set (the ε4 coding tag rs429358 is excluded from the instrument set by design) applied to IAMDGC late AMD gave IVW β = +0.213 (SE = 0.040, 95% CI 0.135 to 0.291, P = 8.5 × 10⁻⁸), MR-Egger β = +0.177 (P = 0.023; intercept P = 0.59) and weighted median β = +0.189 (P = 1.6 × 10⁻⁴) — the same direction and comparable magnitude as the primary AMD estimate. Applied to Jansen 2019 AD it gave IVW β = −0.177 (SE = 0.027, 95% CI −0.230 to −0.125, P = 3.9 × 10⁻¹¹), MR-Egger β = −0.181 (P = 5.2 × 10⁻⁴; intercept P = 0.93) and weighted median β = −0.136 (P = 1.1 × 10⁻⁸) — the same direction as the primary AD estimate. Single-SNP ε4 tag direction gates passed in both files before any MR was run (IAMDGC C→T β = −0.384, P = 2.1 × 10⁻⁴¹: ε4 protects from AMD; Jansen C→T β = +0.162, P = 10⁻³⁰⁰: ε4 is an AD risk), and the ε2 tag (rs7412) was directionally mirror-consistent in both (IAMDGC T→C β = +0.155, P = 1.4 × 10⁻⁶: ε2 raises AMD risk; Jansen T→C β = −0.088, P = 4.7 × 10⁻¹⁰⁰: ε2 lowers AD risk). Steiger directionality tests supported the APOE-protein-level → disease direction over reverse causation in both new cohorts (IAMDGC z = 47.2, P < 10⁻³⁰⁰; Jansen z = 58.9, P < 10⁻³⁰⁰, both forward). The 19-protein APOE-family/complement negative-control panel was re-run on the two new cohorts (Supplementary Table S1d): APOE was again the only protein to pass the panel-Bonferroni threshold (P × 19 < 0.05) on both outcomes (IAMDGC P = 8.5 × 10⁻⁸; Jansen P = 3.9 × 10⁻¹¹), with complement factor D significant on IAMDGC AMD only (β = +0.51, P = 8.8 × 10⁻⁶, panel-Bonferroni P = 1.7 × 10⁻⁴) — the complement AMD-specificity pattern observed in the FinnGen R13 panel is preserved on the independent cohorts.

**AD second validation and sensitivity comparisons (non-independent or underpowered).** The Bellenguez 2022 analysis (which contains the FinnGen component; β = −0.764, P = 7.1 × 10⁻¹⁷⁰) and the Kunkle 2019 analysis (35,274 cases and 59,163 controls; total N = 94,437; includes ε4; β = −1.478, P = 1.5 × 10⁻¹⁸¹) are reported as sensitivity comparisons because Kunkle is a component of the Bellenguez meta-analysis. The UK Biobank AMD analysis (ICD-coded, N ≈ 30,000) was underpowered (ε4 Wald ratio P = 0.079), owing to low case counts, phenotype misclassification, and the absence of the clumped APOE instruments from the UKB imputation panel; it is treated as a sensitivity comparison, not as evidence against the effect.

**Exposure-side validation with deCODE (Icelandic Olink).** We extended the causal inference to a non-UK, non-Finnish exposure source for cohort/ancestry confirmation (direction and magnitude, not instrument-diversity replication). APOE cis-pQTL instruments were derived from the deCODE Icelandic Olink proteomics cohort (N = 35,367–35,372 per SNP in the released per-SNP file), which shares no participants with the FinnGen R13 AMD/AD outcome cohorts. We applied the same IVW / MR-Egger / weighted median estimators (Supplementary Table S12). For **AD**, the deCODE instruments recovered a strong protective effect: IVW β = −0.898 (SE = 0.274, P = 0.001; weighted median β = −1.019, P = 2.0 × 10⁻³⁴; MR-Egger β = −2.87, P = 2.2 × 10⁻⁵), concordant in direction and order of magnitude with the primary UKB-PPP GSMR (β = −0.942, P = 4.1 × 10⁻¹⁸). For **IAMDGC late AMD**, the deCODE instruments gave IVW β = +0.131 (SE = 0.065, P = 0.043; weighted median β = +0.095, P = 0.14; MR-Egger not significant), directionally concordant with the primary AMD signal, though underpowered for a formal claim. For **AMD in the FinnGen R13 discovery cohort**, the deCODE instruments gave IVW β = +0.078 (SE = 0.061, P = 0.20; weighted median β = +0.097, P = 0.071), directionally concordant but non-significant owing to the small deCODE instrument set (six SNPs). Two single-SNP directional checks confirmed concordance on both disease axes (rs71352239: AMD β = +0.101, P = 0.079; AD β = −1.176, P = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰³; IAMDGC β = +0.077, P = 0.25). Because the deCODE instrument set derives from the same APOE cis-locus as the primary UKB-PPP set and does not penalise the ε4 coding cluster, it constitutes a non-UK cohort/ancestry confirmation of the causal direction, not an independent-instrument replication; the two are not statistically independent evidence.

**Per-SNP analysis within the same deCODE assay.** We repeated the MR at per-SNP resolution **in the same deCODE Icelandic Olink assay** used for the exposure-side replication above (N = 35,367–35,372 per SNP; best cis-pQTL P = 1.96 × 10⁻¹⁷¹). This is not an independent proteomic platform: the per-SNP effect sizes and the clumped six-instrument set derive from the same deCODE release (the tag-SNP estimates are numerically identical between the two analyses; revision/results/r13/crosssource_concordance.csv, note column), so the analysis tests instrument-selection robustness within the deCODE source rather than cross-platform robustness. Using the identical 11-instrument set adopted for the clumped primary analysis (r² = 0.001, 10 Mb), the AD direction held in the discovery cohort (IVW β = −2.127, P = 0.024; weighted median β = −2.540, P = 1.3 × 10⁻⁹), while the AMD direction was concordant but non-significant (IVW β = +0.203, P = 0.353; weighted median β = +0.294, P = 0.083); the same pattern held in IAMDGC (all three estimators non-significant; IVW β = +0.236 and weighted median β = +0.266 positive, MR-Egger β = −0.375). Three single-SNP tag checks from the same source gave concordant directions for rs429358 (ε4 tag; e.g., AD Wald β = −4.464, P = 4.2 × 10⁻⁸⁶) and rs71352239 (e.g., AD Wald β = −1.176, P = 1.6 × 10⁻⁶⁵), while rs7412 (the ε2 tag) opposes protein levels by expectation, consistent with the mirror-allele design. Magnitudes are not directly comparable across exposure sources because the two sources quantify on different scales (e.g., the ε4 tag β = −0.2228 in deCODE versus −1.012 in UKB-PPP); the comparison is therefore directional and significance-based. Full numbers in Supplementary Table S15a,b.

### APOE specificity and tissue of action (Supplementary Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. 3)

To test whether the opposite effects are APOE-specific or a property of the broader lipoprotein/complement proteome, we ran the same MR design for a 19-protein panel: the APOE family (APOA1, APOA4, APOB*, APOC1, APOC2*, APOC3*, APOC4*, APOC5*, APOH, APOBEC1*, LPA, LPL, GPIHBP1, LIPC*), complement factors (C3, CFD, CFH, C9), and APOE itself (* = no clumped UKB-PPP instrument available; reported as such). Only APOE showed robust opposite-direction effects on both diseases (AMD IVW β = +0.182, 95% CI +0.127 to +0.237, p = 7.9 × 10⁻¹¹; AD IVW β = −0.942, 95% CI −1.155 to −0.729, p = 4.1 × 10⁻¹⁸; Supplementary Fig. 2). APOE was also the only protein to pass a panel-level Bonferroni threshold on both outcomes (0.05/22 = 2.3 × 10⁻³). Among the complement proteins, CFH (AMD β = −0.674, p = 6.4 × 10⁻³⁴) and C3 (AMD β = +0.550, p = 4.8 × 10⁻⁷) acted on AMD only, consistent with their established AMD biology[28,29], while CFD was not estimable in the R13 outcome files; APOC1, which lies immediately adjacent to APOE and is in LD with it, showed an AD association in the opposite direction (β = +0.96, p = 0.021) that we do not interpret as independent evidence. All other proteins were non-significant in at least one disease (full panel in Supplementary Table S8). A convergent proteome-MR study identified the same APOE-specificity pattern across the plasma proteome[13]; our additional contribution is that the opposite direction (AMD risk↑ / AD risk↓) on the **same** APOE molecule—rather than a single-disease target—is what, to our knowledge, has not been reported before in a causal-MR, two-disease framing.

A central question for the level model is *where* APOE abundance acts. We tested retinal tissue directly with retinal eQTL instruments in MR. Retinal APOE eQTL are intrinsically weak: in Strunz 2020 (n = 311, mega-analysis of retinal RNA-seq) the best APOE cis-eQTL reached only P = 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ (F ≈ 13 for the top-10 set), and in EyeGEx (n = 129) four variants reached nominal significance with one at P = 5.3 × 10⁻⁸ (F ≈ 27). Using these instruments (Supplementary Fig. 3): (i) retinal APOE expression → AMD was null with the EyeGEx set (IVW β = −0.0003, p = 0.88; Egger/WMed concordant nulls) and non-robust with the weak Strunz set (IVW β = −0.055, p = 0.26; WMed β = −0.131, p = 0.0099, direction unstable across methods); (ii) retinal APOE expression → AD was nominally significant with EyeGEx (IVW β = +0.0105, p = 4 × 10⁻⁷⁹) but ~70-fold smaller in magnitude than the plasma effect (−0.763) and opposite in sign, a pattern consistent with LD confounding with the ε4 functional cluster rather than a retinal-expression effect; the weak Strunz set again gave an unstable, small-magnitude estimate (IVW β = −0.041, p = 7.2 × 10⁻⁴). Steiger tests supported the eQTL→disease direction (AMD p = 5.3 × 10⁻⁷; AD p = 1.4 × 10⁻⁶; full results in Supplementary Table S9). GTEx v11 provided no APOE eQTL in any brain tissue at P < 5 × 10⁻⁸, so brain-tissue MR was not feasible (reported as a data limitation).

Read together with the null stage-stratified scRNA-seq result (below), the tissue contrast indicates that the causal variable captured by the pQTL design is **systemic (plasma) APOE abundance, not retinal APOE expression**: the retinal tissue does not show a robust APOE-abundance → AMD effect, while the plasma level does (β = +0.182 in the discovery cohort).

### Cellular and protein-level context (Figure 5A–D, Supplementary Fig. 7)

**Retinal expression of the disease-associated protein genes.** To complement the enrichment frameworks with a direct, descriptive view, we quantified the expression of all 14 disease-associated protein genes across the six retinal cell types profiled in GSE230348 (Supplementary Table S26). APOE is by far the most abundant transcript in this tissue (Müller glia: log-normalised mean 3.16, expressed in 82% of cells; microglia 1.63/54%; RPE 1.26/57%), which is consistent with the cell-type relevance rankings below. By contrast, the complement genes whose protein levels carry AMD risk are essentially not transcribed in these retinal cells (CFHR5 0.00%, CFHR4 0.01%, CFHR2 0.10% of cells), and ACE, EPHA1, C2 and C3 are likewise low (0.1-28% of cells), which is consistent with a systemic rather than locally synthesised source of the plasma proteins measured in the MR analysis; CFH (RPE, 23%), CFD (microglia, 48%) and CFI (Müller glia, 44%) are the exceptions. These are descriptive expression statistics, not enrichment tests.

To localize the disease associations at cellular resolution, we applied two independent GWAS × single-cell frameworks to the 75-sample, four-stage AMD retina snRNA-seq dataset (GSE230348; 92,439 scored cells, six cell types): scDRS (gene-set cell scoring with permutation-calibrated FPR)[30] and seismic (cell-type specificity × MAGMA gene-Z regression with driver-gene decomposition)[31] (Supplementary Table S10a,b).

**scDRS.** For AD, microglia were the most disease-relevant cell type (mean z = +1.64; rank-biserial vs all other cells = −0.88; Mann–Whitney P underflowed to 0 at double precision, BH-FDR < 10⁻³⁰⁰), consistent with the established microglial role in AD. For AMD, relevance was concentrated in the retina–RPE interface: RPE (mean z = −2.02; FDR = 6.6 × 10⁻²⁹) and endothelial cells (FDR = 5.5 × 10⁻³³), with Müller glia and rod cells also significant (all FDR < 0.05; Supplementary Fig. 7A). APOE expression itself was highest in Müller glia (mean 29.3) and microglia (10.2), the two cell types that top the AMD and AD relevance rankings respectively.

**seismic.** Cell-type–trait association testing identified endothelial cells as the FDR-significant AMD-associated type (FDR = 0.040) and microglia as the FDR-significant AD-associated type (FDR = 0.027; Supplementary Fig. 7B). In the driver-gene decomposition, **APOE was identified as an influential gene for the endothelial–AMD association (dfβ = +0.072) and for the Müller-glia–AD association (dfβ = +0.028)** — directly linking the protein of interest to cell-type-specific disease architecture in both diseases.

**Stage-stratified expression (GSE230348).** Pseudobulk APOE mRNA showed no significant change with disease stage in the full retina (Normal → Early-Intermediate → Intermediate → Atrophic; slope −0.76 per stage unit, P = 0.11; 67 samples with usable stage assignment), and tissue-level trends were underpowered and directionally inconsistent (all P > 0.03). These data, together with the null retinal eQTL MR above, argue against retinal APOE transcription being the causal variable in the MR design.

**Disease-protein colocalization.** We tested 16 disease-associated proteins individually (coloc.abf against the FinnGen R13 disease signals; Supplementary Table S23). Only APOE and PILRA shared a causal variant with the disease signal (PP4 = 1.00 and 0.997 for AD; 1.00 and 0.94 for AMD). Ten proteins were attributed to causal variants distinct from the AMD signal (CFHR5, CFHR4, CFHR2, CFB, C2, CDSN, DXO, F13B, HLA-DRA, HLA-E; PP3 ~ 1, with r² between the protein and disease leads of 0.013-0.535) and four to variants distinct from the AD signal (CFB, C2, DXO, HLA-DRA); the remaining protein-disease pairs were inconclusive (PP3 0.01-0.61). Because the local UKB-PPP file is a 5 x 10^-8-thresholded set, these PP3 values are lead-variant/LD evidence rather than full-region colocalization; CFD could not be tested (no overlap between its cis variants and the disease files), and the distinct-variant attribution does not weaken the MR estimates, which use multiple independent instruments.

**Tissue-level SMR in retina.** Summary-data-based MR using retinal cis-eQTL instruments gave a null APOE-expression effect on AMD (b_SMR = -0.0013, P = 0.41, FDR = 0.87; IAMDGC P = 0.53) and a nominally significant effect on AD (b_SMR = -0.0146, P = 1.09 x 10^-6, FDR = 5.7 x 10^-4) that must be interpreted with caution because HEIDI could not be evaluated (a single cis-eQTL instrument). The AMD null supports the systemic (plasma) rather than a retina-local model of APOE abundance, consistent with the tissue contrast above; the AD estimate is directionally consistent with the plasma effect but is not independent evidence (Supplementary Table S24a,b).

**Cross-disease protein network (Figure 5D).** Across the 675 proteins with GSMR estimates in both diseases, 356 (53%) had opposite-signed effects. In the proteome-scan FDR family (16 AMD- and 6 AD-associated proteins), only two - APOE and C2 - were significant in both with opposite signs (APOE: b_AMD = +0.181, HEIDI pass; b_AD = -0.786, HEIDI fail; C2: +0.494 / -0.325, HEIDI fail on both sides; C2's AD-side association does not survive a BH correction computed within the 675-protein cross-disease set, where the counts are 8 and 3 and C2's AD BH P = 0.30). No protein was significant on both sides with HEIDI passing on both, so the "only protein with opposite causal effects" claim rests on the stricter Bonferroni threshold (8 AMD and 3 AD proteins) and on APOE's AMD-side HEIDI pass; C2 is a directional corroboration only (Supplementary Table S25). A protein × cell-type map based on the seismic gene sets covered only 4 of the 14 disease-associated proteins (APOE, CFH, CFD, CFI), so it is reported as exploratory only (`revision/results/mechanism/celltype_protein_map.csv`).

### Mechanism of the switch: cell-type targets, the receptor axis and the complement pathway (Figure 5)

**The complement pathway is a co-regulated, not an APOE-driven, programme.** Complement-pathway proteins were over-represented among AMD risk-increasing proteins in the pQTL GSMR set (GSEA on the 675-protein ranking: NES = +1.92, P = 8.7 × 10⁻⁴, BH-adjusted P = 2.6 × 10⁻³ for the KEGG complement list; NES = +1.67, nominal P = 0.033 for the MSigDB C2:CP:KEGG 2023.1 complement-and-coagulation set, which does not survive correction across the KEGG sets that pass the size filter (15 sets with at least 10 measured proteins; 35 when the filter is relaxed to 5), BH-adjusted P = 0.16, because only 16 of its 69 genes have GSMR estimates; no enrichment on the AD side, NES = −0.70). We treat the pathway analysis as supportive context rather than primary evidence: five complement proteins (CFHR5, CFHR4, CFHR2, CFB and C2) are themselves genome-wide significant in the AMD scan (Supplementary Table S1), consistent with the foundational CFH/complement discoveries in AMD[28,29]. Cross-tissue GSMR identified NFKB1 (β = +0.40, P = 3.8 × 10⁻⁴) in muscle skeletal eQTL as exploratory pathway evidence; standard multivariable MR could not be run because the APOE pQTL and NFKB1 GTEx eQTL instruments do not overlap, so the APOE-NFKB1 axis is not presented as formal multivariable MR evidence. **APOE→complement test (zero overlap).** Using deCODE APOE pQTL as the exposure (6 cis-pQTL instruments, mean F = 175) and UKB-PPP complement pQTLs as outcomes, genetically predicted APOE level did not alter any of eight complement proteins (IVW P = 0.13–0.77; MR-Egger intercept P > 0.24; Cochran's Q P = 0.47–0.94). The complement association is therefore co-regulated with, not driven by, the APOE level.

**The APOE receptor axis diverges between the two diseases (Figure 5A).** Cell-type expression of the APOE receptor and lipid-transport genes (GSE230348; Supplementary Table S26; `revision/results/mechanism/apoe_receptor_axis.csv`) shows a clean split. On the AD side, the microglial immune/efferocytosis receptors dominate: TREM2 is expressed in 32.9% of microglia, PLAUR in 53.0%, AXL in 32.5% and MERTK in 31.7%, each in fewer than 17% of RPE or endothelial cells. On the AMD side, the RPE/endothelial lipid-uptake and barrier-transport machinery dominates: LDLR in 46.9% of RPE cells, LRP8 in 22.5% and ABCB1 in 33.6% of endothelial cells, each in fewer than 30% of microglia. TREM2 is itself an AD-side MR hit in this study (Jansen 2019, Supplementary Table S1b), so the AD-side receptor arm connects the causal protein to a disease-associated microglial receptor rather than to a generic marker.

**The APOE co-expression programme differs by cell type and carries the complement signal (Figure 5C).** To avoid the library-size and cell-quality artefacts that dominate raw co-expression, we computed partial correlations of APOE with every gene within each cell type, controlling for log total UMI and log detected genes (`revision/results/mechanism/apoe_coexpression_official_*.csv`). In microglia the strongest partners are APOC1 (+0.72), SPP1 (+0.72) and TREM2 (+0.54); in endothelial cells they are SPP1 (+0.49), APOC1 (+0.47), C3 (+0.41) and C1QC (+0.37). Pathway enrichment of these deconfounded programmes (`revision/results/mechanism/apoe_coexpression_pathways_ppcor_allgenes.csv`) recapitulates the cross-layer complement split: the complement-and-coagulation cascade is enriched in the AMD-side cell types (endothelial NES = +2.06, padj = 7.7 × 10⁻⁶; rod NES = +1.85, padj = 2.4 × 10⁻⁴) and depleted in the AD-side cell types (microglia NES = −1.66, padj = 0.017; Müller glia NES = −2.05, padj = 2.9 × 10⁻⁶). Because the APOE→complement MR test above is null, we read this as a co-regulated complement programme that follows the cell type rather than as APOE driving complement activation.

**Ligand–receptor communication converges on microglial TREM2 (Supplementary Table S30).** CellChat analysis of the same matrix (CellChatDB.human; triMean communication probabilities; min.cells = 10; `revision/105_cellchat_apoe.R`) identified the APOE ligand–receptor pair annotated in the database as **APOE → TREM2–TYROBP** (ApoE pathway). Signalling is almost exclusively directed to microglia: Müller glia → microglia 0.0935, RPE → microglia 0.0350 and microglial autocrine 0.0346, with zero communication strength to any other target cell type (`revision/results/mechanism/cellchat_APOE_TREM2_TYROBP.csv`). This is consistent, from a third data layer, with the receptor axis (TREM2 restricted to microglia) and with the microglial APOE co-expression partner TREM2 (+0.54).

**The cell-type result is not driven by the APOE region.** The single-cell gene sets are MAGMA-derived and could be circular for APOE. We therefore repeated scDRS after removing all 58 genes in the APOE region (chr19:44–46 Mb): microglia remained the top-ranked AD cell type (mean z +1.69 without the region versus +1.64 with it included; rank-biserial +0.862 versus +0.869), and the AMD gene set contains no APOE-region gene, so the AMD rankings are numerically unchanged (`revision/results/scdrs_apoe/scdrs_celltype_tests_noAPOEregion.csv`). At the genetic level, the APOE region and the genome-wide background behave differently: the correlation of APOE-region effect sizes between AMD and AD is r = −0.463 (989 LD-pruned SNPs; 60.2% opposite-sign; binomial P = 1.76 × 10⁻¹⁰), whereas the genome-wide HapMap3 correlation is r = −0.030 (139,020 LD-pruned SNPs; 50.8% opposite-sign; binomial P = 3.08 × 10⁻¹⁰) (`revision/results/mechanism/region_vs_genome_effect_correlation.csv`). Using the official method for regional local genetic correlation (LAVA 0.1.5 on the EU1000QC reference; chr19:44–46 Mb, 5,187 alignable SNPs), the APOE region itself carries a strong negative local genetic correlation — **rho = −0.652, 95% CI −0.74 to −0.57, simulation P = 3.4 × 10⁻⁴¹** — whereas three equally sized non-APOE control windows on the same chromosome were non-significant (P = 0.33, 0.074 and 0.97; `lava_scan.csv`), and among the 19 estimable tiled 2-Mb windows of chromosome 19 the APOE window was the most significant (`lava_chr19_windows.csv`).

**Synthesis.** Across four independent data layers — plasma pQTL MR, retinal expression, single-cell co-expression and ligand–receptor communication — the same APOE molecule carries two different downstream programmes: a microglial TREM2/immune axis associated with AD protection when APOE is high, and an RPE/endothelial lipid-transport axis associated with AMD risk when APOE is high, with the complement cascade following the AMD-side programme rather than being caused by APOE. A network-level virtual perturbation of APOE was attempted to test whether the co-expression programmes depend on APOE's own regulatory output, and is reported as an **exploratory negative** (Supplementary Table S32). Using scTenifoldPerturb 0.1.3 on one reconstructed wild-type network per cell type, with APOE's outgoing edges zeroed, the perturbation acted exactly as specified, but the output was not interpretable at gene level for two reasons. First, only 0–3 genes beyond APOE itself reached FDR < 0.05 in any cell type. Second, the identity of the top genes was not reproducible across parameter settings: against a stability threshold fixed before the comparison (top-20 overlap ≥ 50%, Spearman ≥ 0.70 between network depths), RPE (1/20 overlap, 5%; ρ = 0.29) and endothelium (1/20; ρ = 0.64) both failed, and only microglia passed (14/20; ρ = 0.81). The differential-regulation statistic is a distance in the aligned manifold rather than a signed effect, so it tests dependence on APOE's regulatory output and cannot assign a direction. We therefore do not interpret individual genes. A higher-depth rerun (10 networks of 500 cells per cell type) reproduced the null, and a stability comparison across the shallower and deeper runs is reported in Supplementary Table S32.

### Translational implications: a direction-split APOE pipeline (Figure 6)

**Drug target prioritization.** Cross-disease proteome-wide comparison identified several targets with opposite or disease-specific causal effects (Supplementary Table S3). Complement factor D (CFD) is an AMD-specific target with no detectable AD effect (IAMDGC first-validation cohort: AMD β = +0.51, FDR = 7.6 × 10⁻⁴; Jansen 2019 AD: β = −0.001, P = 0.96); CFD is not estimable in the FinnGen R13 discovery scan. Danicopan, a factor D inhibitor approved for extravascular haemolysis in paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria, was tested in a single phase 2 geographic-atrophy trial (NCT05019521) that was terminated for lack of efficacy, so the direction predicted here has not been supported clinically. Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) was associated with lower AD risk (GSMR β = −0.132, FDR = 5.9 × 10⁻³) with no AMD effect (β = +0.020, FDR = 0.89); because the exposure is ACE protein level, this direction implies that **lowering** ACE — the pharmacological action of ACE inhibitors — would be predicted to *increase* AD risk, so ACE inhibition is not supported as an AD-prevention strategy by these data. The same caveat applies to B-cell activating factor (TNFSF13B/BAFF), for which higher levels were associated with lower AD risk (β = −0.086, FDR = 0.74, i.e. not significant after correction); belimumab lowers BAFF and would therefore be predicted to act in the unfavourable direction. These two proteins are reported as hypothesis-generating directional observations only, and the drug-direction inference is left open.

**Pharmacovigilance was not supportive of drug repurposing.** Using strict Alzheimer-type dementia MedDRA PTs and AMD-specific PTs, FAERS (2020 Q1–2026 Q1, 25 quarterly files; 10.75 million reports) contained 5,002 AD and 3,290 AMD reports (report versions; 4,025 and 2,438 unique cases after CASEID deduplication). In the age≥50 suspect-role stratum, infliximab had no AMD events and no significant AD protection (ROR = 1.00, CI 0.67–1.50). TNF inhibitors as a class showed an AD ROR of 1.28 (CI 1.09–1.50) and an AMD ROR of 0.72 (CI 0.56–0.94), but individual TNFi estimates were heterogeneous and negative controls such as acetaminophen showed protective AD RORs, indicating residual confounding and reporting bias. CVARD matching was repaired (AD = 560, AMD = 421 reports) but event counts were too small for reliable ROR estimates. These analyses (Supplementary Table S5) do not support the previous claim that infliximab or TNF inhibition protects against AMD and AD, and drug repurposing conclusions are downgraded to candidate targets requiring clinical validation.

**A translational gap with a direction constraint (Figure 6).** APOE-directed approaches are under development: valiltramiprosate targets Aβ aggregation in APOE4 homozygotes (Phase III, APOLLOE4; completed)[32]; LX1001 delivers **APOE2** by intrathecal in vivo gene therapy (Phase 1/2; NCT03634007, completed, with long-term follow-up NCT05400330)[33]; and anti-APOE4 antibody approaches remain largely preclinical[34]. None currently has a registered trial with AMD as a primary or secondary endpoint. An APOE-lowering antisense oligonucleotide (RO7812653, Roche/Ionis) entered phase 1 in 2026 and is designed to reduce APOE in the CNS but not in peripheral tissues (NCT07234942), so the level-model prediction below is a direction-dependent forecast that is now prospectively testable rather than a read-out of an ongoing ophthalmic study. Under the level model the predicted effect is direction-dependent: an approach that **lowers** circulating APOE abundance would be expected to **reduce** AMD risk, whereas one that **raises** APOE abundance (e.g., APOE2 gene delivery such as LX1001) would be expected to **increase** AMD risk, and gene delivery should be read in terms of its net effect on circulating APOE. Because the sign of the predicted AMD effect depends on each therapy's effect on APOE abundance, APOE-directed AD development should monitor both ophthalmic endpoints and circulating APOE levels. Figure 6 places each agent on the abundance axis; the positions are model-assumed, not measured drug effects, and no agent is designed to lower circulating APOE.

## Discussion

The ε4 allele reduces APOE protein abundance by ~64%, and APOE abundance has opposite causal effects on AMD (higher = risk) and AD (higher = protection). The same biochemical event therefore produces opposite disease outcomes, resolving the long-standing ε4 paradox. We describe this as inverse comorbidity at the molecular level: the two diseases coexist in ageing populations but do not share a genetic architecture — their genome-wide genetic correlation is negative and indistinguishable from zero (GenomicSEM multivariate LDSC rg = −0.026, SE = 0.091, P = 0.77; standard two-sample LDSC rg = −0.061, SE = 0.072, P = 0.39) — yet at the one locus they do share, the same measurable molecular state, APOE abundance, pushes them in opposite directions. Throughout, "APOE protein abundance" refers to the cis-pQTL-instrumented protein level — the MR exposure defined by the UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project APOE instruments — rather than to a directly measured plasma concentration. The claim is molecular, not epidemiological: we do not estimate co-prevalence or co-incidence, which would require population cohort data that this study does not contain.

Each direction of our result has a published precedent, and those precedents are convergent rather than competing. Rasmussen et al. reported, from Danish cohorts and with APOE allele scores, that genetically lower plasma apoE raises dementia risk (RR 1.41 per 1 mg/dL; 1.27–1.57)[10], and in a separate study that genetically higher plasma apoE raises AMD risk (aHR 1.12 per 1 mg/dL; 1.05–1.19)[11]; Belbasis et al. reported APOE abundance → AD with cis-pQTL instruments (OR 0.61, P = 3.4 × 10⁻⁶⁵, colocalization-supported)[12]; and Hou et al. independently prioritized APOE in dry-AMD pQTL MR (OR 1.19 per s.d.)[13]. Our instruments and cohorts are independent of all four, and reproducing both directions with an instrument definition distinct from an allele score is stronger replication than any single-cohort estimate. The increment is the pairing and the attribution. None of those studies tested one abundance axis against both diseases: Rasmussen's two analyses use the same allele-score design but are separate, disease-specific papers, and Belbasis and Hou each address a single disease. We obtain opposite signs from one instrument-defined exposure, quantify how much of the per-allele ε4 and ε2 effects is level-mediated (90.1% for ε4 and 92.8% for ε2 on the AMD side; 95.8% for ε4 on the AD side), and attribute the divergence to different cell-type programmes and receptor axes. A large multi-cohort proteomic study recently mapped the APOE-ε4 and ε2-driven plasma/CSF proteomic signatures and upstream/downstream statistical mediators in Alzheimer's disease (Lu et al. [16]; >10,000 individuals, SomaLogic and Olink, plasma and CSF). That work is complementary but distinct: it is an observational, AD-only mapping of the APOE-genotype-associated proteome (it explicitly excludes APOE-targeting aptamers, so it does not measure the APOE protein itself), uses statistical mediation rather than genetic-instrument causal estimation, and does not consider AMD. Our contribution differs in kind: we use APOE cis-pQTL instruments to estimate the **causal** effect of APOE protein abundance on the disease endpoint, and we do so for **both** AMD and AD, showing the same molecule carries **opposite** directions (AMD risk↑ / AD risk↓) — a bidirectional trade-off that Lu 2026, being AD-only and non-causal, does not address. The two studies are best read as layered: Lu 2026 maps the allele-driven protein network (which non-APOE proteins change with genotype and their mediator vs downstream status); we resolve the direction and disease-specificity of the APOE protein's own causal effect.

Beyond resolving a single-variant paradox, our findings identify a shared systemic molecular state carrying opposite causal risks for two degenerative diseases of late life. If the APOE level axis is a disease-risk modifier (rather than an incidental property of ε4 carriers), APOE-lowering exposures should shift the two diseases in opposite directions — reducing AD risk while increasing AMD risk — consistent with the direction estimated here (deCODE exposure-side replication: AD IVW β = −0.898, p = 0.001; AMD direction concordant, not significant; Supplementary Table S12). Clinical trials of APOE modulation should therefore pre-specify AMD and AD readouts as paired endpoints and monitor both risks in parallel. Crucially, this direction-dependent constraint is not limited to European-ancestry samples: in the ancestrally diverse Million Veteran Program the APOE→AMD direction reproduced independently (EUR and trans-ancestry meta-analysis P < 10⁻⁶), was direction-concordant in the smaller African-American/Hispanic strata, and was consistent across AMD subtypes (dry and wet; ε4 protection significant for both, and the clumped-instrument estimates in FinnGen R13 are +0.199 for dry and +0.161 for wet AMD, which do not differ from each other, Δβ = 0.038, SE 0.066, P = 0.57), supporting the cross-population and cross-subtype generalisability of the bidirectional constraint. No anti-APOE antibody trial with ophthalmic endpoints is currently registered as of 2026-09-09, so the prediction remains a preclinical forecast for future APOE-directed development. At the target level this is a one-sided problem. The APOE-directed agents now in clinical development act uniformly in the APOE-raising direction — LX1001, which delivers APOE2 intrathecally (NCT03634007, phase 1/2 completed; NCT05400330, long-term follow-up)[33], and ALZ-801, an oral APOE4-conformation modulator that does not change APOE levels (NCT04693520, phase 2; NCT04770220, phase 3, primary endpoint not met)[35] — while an APOE-lowering antisense oligonucleotide (RO7812653; NCT07234942) entered phase 1 in 2026 and Open Targets records no approved or clinical candidate that directly targets APOE (Supplementary Table S29). Our causal model therefore predicts that the agents now furthest along should **raise**, not lower, AMD risk, which makes ophthalmic monitoring a concrete and inexpensive safety readout in exactly those trials; conversely, an APOE-lowering agent would be predicted to reduce AMD risk while increasing AD risk, so the two diseases must be co-monitored whichever direction is pursued.

The concordance between the R13 proteome-wide GSMR (β = +0.181), the R13 per-unit ε4 Wald ratio (β = +0.202), and the clumping-robustness grid (6/6 concordant cells; IVW AMD +0.175 to +0.195) provides robust support for a positive causal effect of APOE on AMD; the AD direction is equally stable across instrument definitions (IVW R13 grid −1.260 to −0.988). In the clumped-instrument R13 analysis, the AMD MR-Egger intercept was not significant (p = 0.567) but the AD intercept was significant (p = 0.006) — consistent with fine-mapping placing the AD signal on the ε4 coding cluster while the level-regulating variants carry the AMD signal — while the AD direction and magnitude remain robust (weighted median β = −0.859, P = 2.4 × 10⁻⁹⁹). The ε4 and ε2 coding variants function as natural human knockdown and overexpression alleles — ε4 lowering APOE abundance by ~64% and ε2 raising it by ~110% per allele (scale note in Methods) — analogous to PCSK9 loss-of-function mutations[36]. The mirror-allele test (Supplementary Table S20) provides an internal consistency check of the level model: the protein-lowering ε4 increases AD risk and lowers AMD risk, while the protein-raising ε2 (pQTL β = +0.745) shows the exact reverse — AD protection (β = −0.523, P = 2.4 × 10⁻⁷¹) and AMD risk (β = +0.146, P = 9.0 × 10⁻⁸ FinnGen R13; β = +0.155, P = 1.4 × 10⁻⁶ IAMDGC) — with all ε2 per-allele effects genome-wide significant, so ε4 and ε2 map onto the same APOE-abundance axis in opposite directions rather than an allele-specific artifact.

Beyond the variant-level fine-mapping (AD signal on the ε4 coding cluster; AMD signal on level-regulating variants), two mechanistic constraints follow from the present work. First, the opposite effects are specific to APOE within the 19-protein lipoprotein/complement panel (complement factors act on AMD only). Second, retinal APOE expression is not the causal variable: retinal eQTL MR is null/unstable for AMD and ~70-fold smaller than the plasma effect for AD, while plasma pQTL MR is strongly significant in both directions. The level model therefore acts through circulating APOE abundance, not local retinal transcription — consistent with the null stage-stratified scRNA-seq result and with APOE's established secretory, lipid-transport biology. The new cell-type and ligand–receptor analyses extend this from "where the protein acts" to "which programme it engages": the APOE receptor arm splits cleanly between a microglial TREM2/PLAUR/AXL/MERTK axis and an RPE/endothelial LDLR/LRP8/ABCB1 axis; CellChat assigns essentially all APOE→TREM2–TYROBP signalling to microglia as the target (Müller glia → microglia 0.0935; RPE → microglia 0.0350; microglial autocrine 0.0346); and the deconfounded APOE co-expression programme carries a complement signature that is enriched in the AMD-side cell types (endothelial NES = +2.06, padj = 7.7 × 10⁻⁶; rod NES = +1.85, padj = 2.4 × 10⁻⁴) and depleted in the AD-side microglia (NES = −1.66, padj = 0.017). Because the zero-overlap APOE→complement MR test is null (IVW P = 0.13–0.77), we interpret complement as co-regulated with, rather than caused by, APOE level. These single-cell layers are descriptive and correlational; they localise a programme, they do not establish that APOE drives it.

Single-cell resolution (scDRS + seismic, two independent methods on the same matrix) localized AD relevance to microglia (rank-biserial −0.88; seismic FDR 0.027) and AMD relevance to the RPE/endothelial retina–choroid interface (seismic endothelial FDR 0.040), with APOE emerging as a driver gene of the endothelial–AMD and Müller-glia–AD associations. APOE expression peaks in Müller glia (29.3) and microglia (10.2) — the top cell types for the AMD and AD relevance rankings, respectively. Cell-type enrichment of the proteome-wide gene set did not identify an FDR-significant single type (reported as honest negative), so we frame these results as cellular context, not cellular mediation. Because the scDRS gene sets are MAGMA-derived and could be circular for APOE, we repeated the analysis with all 58 APOE-region genes removed; microglia remained the top AD cell type (mean z +1.69 versus +1.64), so the cell-type ranking is not an APOE-gene-set artefact.

The diametrically opposed APOE effects at both protein and coding levels are consistent with the tiered colocalization results: the AMD signal sits on the level-regulator block (PP4 = 0.974 with the ε4 coding tag excluded from the instrument set), while the AD signal additionally colocalizes with the level instrument set at single-signal resolution (PP4 = 1.000 with the full set and 0.996 with the ε4-independent clumped set; the independent-cohort descriptive Jansen 2019 z-profile shows the same ε4-cluster dominance). Fine-mapping resolves the within-locus attribution: the AD signal is concentrated on the ε4 coding cluster (PIP = 1.0 at 45.40–45.42 Mb), while the level-regulating variants that drive AMD carry PIP ≈ 0 in AD (Supplementary Fig. 5). Because ε4 is the strongest regulator of APOE abundance (β = −1.012 per allele), this pattern is consistent with a predominantly level-mediated AD mechanism; a superimposed isoform-specific component of ε4 remains possible but is not separable from the level effect here. The negative genetic correlation (GenomicSEM R13: rg = −0.026, P = 0.77; standard LDSC: rg = −0.061, P = 0.39) is directionally consistent with opposing APOE-level effects at a shared locus; at the variant level the APOE region itself carries a strong negative local genetic correlation (LAVA rho = −0.652, 95% CI −0.74 to −0.57, P = 3.4 × 10⁻⁴¹) against a null genome-wide correlation, which is what a locus-restricted trade-off predicts. No common-factor model is fitted, because two traits cannot identify one (df = −1; Methods), so the genetic relationship between the diseases is summarised by rg alone.

After clumping to independent instruments in the FinnGen R13 discovery cohort, the AMD MR-Egger intercept was not significant (p = 0.567) whereas the AD intercept was (p = 0.006), and the AD GSMR scan failed the HEIDI heterogeneity test (8 of 11 instruments retained) — both consistent with fine-mapping placing the AD signal on the ε4 coding cluster rather than on the level-regulating variants that carry the AMD signal. The AD direction and magnitude nevertheless remain robust under the weighted-median estimator (β = −0.859, P = 2.4 × 10⁻⁹⁹) and in the independent Jansen 2019 cohort (IVW β = −0.177, P = 3.9 × 10⁻¹¹). Sample overlap: the UKB-PPP exposure (pQTL) partially shares UK Biobank participants with the two AD validation cohorts (Jansen 2019 and Bellenguez 2022 both include UK Biobank proxy-phenotype samples), whereas the discovery cohort (FinnGen R13) is overlap-free; the concordant protective direction in the overlap-carrying cohorts argues that the overlap does not drive the direction (see Limitations). For AMD, neither the discovery cohort (FinnGen R13) nor the first validation (IAMDGC late) contains UK Biobank participants, so no exposure–outcome overlap exists on the AMD side.

A single slope per disease reproduces the ε4 and ε2 heterozygous contrasts within ~5% for three of the four contrasts (with the exception of ε2–AD, which is over-predicted by 16%) (AMD predicted 0.83 vs observed 0.82; AD 2.59 vs 2.70 for ε4; AMD 1.15 vs 1.16 and AD 0.50 vs 0.59 for ε2), which is why the same abundance axis explains both mirrors. The linear form is not, however, validated at two copies: across ten published genotype-stratified strata the observed homozygote odds ratio divided by the additive prediction gives a pooled ratio of 1.13 (95% CI 0.98–1.31, P = 0.10) with substantial heterogeneity (Q = 16.7, P = 0.054) and a deviation direction that reverses across onset strata. The abundance axis should therefore be read as a directional mechanism — the sign of the effect is well anchored, the dose response is not — and we do not use it to predict the magnitude of a pharmacological perturbation. No APOE-directed therapy has been tested with AMD or Alzheimer's disease as an endpoint, so the forecast cannot be calibrated against an interventional anchor; the three assumptions of the forecast (linearity in Δlog APOE; equivalence between a drug-induced change and a lifelong genetic difference of the same magnitude; no effect modification) are stated in the Figure 5 legend, and the abundance axis is a model forecast, not a causal estimate.

If the level model is correct, the AMD risk consequence of an APOE-directed therapy is direction-dependent on its effect on circulating APOE abundance: APOE-lowering approaches are predicted to reduce AMD risk, while APOE-level-raising approaches (including APOE gene delivery) are predicted to increase it. The APOE-directed pipeline is split by direction and compartment. LX1001 delivers APOE2 intrathecally (NCT03634007, phase 1/2 completed; NCT05400330, long-term follow-up)[33]; ALZ-801 is an oral APOE4-conformation modulator that does **not** change APOE levels and whose phase 3 primary endpoint was not met (NCT04693520, phase 2; NCT04770220, phase 3)[35]; and the antisense oligonucleotide RO7812653 (Roche/Ionis) is designed to lower APOE in the CNS but not in peripheral tissues and entered phase 1 in 2026 (NCT07234942). **No agent is designed to lower circulating APOE**, which is the exposure on which our MR estimates are built, so the level-model prediction remains untested in the systemic compartment; the CNS-directed lowering agent nevertheless provides a first prospective test of the predicted direction (lower APOE → lower AMD risk) if it also lowers plasma APOE. Open Targets lists no registered drug or clinical candidate that directly targets APOE, and the pharmacogenomic safety liabilities recorded for APOE are lipid-related (elevated triglycerides, hypertriglyceridaemia, elevated plasma lipids) and haemorrhage (Supplementary Table S29). We therefore suggest that APOE-directed AD trials monitor both circulating APOE levels and ophthalmic endpoints — a cross-disease prediction that is falsifiable in ongoing and future APOE-directed development (Figure 6).

Several limitations qualify these conclusions. First, while Mendelian randomization can support causal inference under specific assumptions (relevance, independence, exclusion restriction), it does not prove causation; our triangulation across MR methods, Steiger directionality, replication, and the clumping grid strengthens but cannot complete the causal case. Second, the ε4 coding variant is simultaneously the strongest regulator of APOE abundance and a protein-coding variant, so a level effect and an isoform-specific effect of ε4 on AD cannot be separated in these data. Fine-mapping places the AD signal on the ε4 coding cluster (PIP = 1.0), which is consistent with a level effect dominated by ε4 (the strongest level regulator), while the separate single-signal colocalization of the level instrument set with AD (PP4 = 1.000 with the full set and 0.996 with the ε4-independent clumped set) speaks against a purely isoform-specific mechanism. We therefore report the AD arm as level-mediated with a possible non-separable isoform-specific component. This remains a limitation to be addressed by larger or alternative instrumental approaches (e.g., isoform-resolved proteomics). A multi-signal (suSiE-based) colocalization was attempted for this contrast and is not reported, because it does not resolve independent signals in this region: every credible set collapsed to a single variant, the 1000 Genomes European LD matrix was not positive semidefinite, the summary-statistic/LD consistency was only s_rss = 0.68 (pQTL) and 0.71 (AD), and two retained variants were in perfect LD (r² = 1.0); the AD outcome is measured in a Finnish founder population and the exposure in UK Biobank, so both depart from the reference LD structure. In addition, the AD GSMR scan did not pass the HEIDI heterogeneity test (8 of 11 instruments retained; Jansen 2019 likewise 7 of 11), so the AD estimate is best read as a direction and magnitude robust to estimator choice (weighted median β = −0.859, P = 2.4 × 10⁻⁹⁹) rather than as a single-causal-variant result. Third, both AD validation cohorts partially share UK Biobank participants with the UKB-PPP exposure (Jansen 2019 and Bellenguez 2022 include UK Biobank proxy-phenotype samples), whereas the discovery cohort FinnGen R13 is overlap-free; the FinnGen-independent AD validation is provided by Jansen 2019 (European; 71,880 clinical + proxy cases; independent of FinnGen (the AD-by-proxy component derives from UK Biobank parental history, so it shares sampling with the UKB-PPP pQTL exposure)), and the two AD validation tiers are not mutually independent either, because both draw on UK Biobank AD-by-proxy participants; with three caveats: (i) the AD phenotype is partly clinical-dementia/proxy-based, which may dilute the molecularly defined AD signal; (ii) the per-allele effect scale in the converted summary file is ~8-fold smaller than FinnGen/Kunkle (likely an imputation/dosage-scale property), so Jansen enters as directional/significance validation only; (iii) the Jansen 2019 no-UKB verification (author list/Methods and file stratification vs the paper supplementary) is a pre-submission human check. The IAMDGC late AMD cohort (16,144 late cases) adds an independent AMD replication; 7 flipped complement-region variants (chr1/chr10) in the IAMDGC file are non-APOE and do not affect the APOE result. Fourth, retinal eQTL for APOE are intrinsically weak (best P = 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ in the largest cohort), so tissue-specific MR is underpowered; our null/weak retinal results should be read as "no evidence for a retinal level effect," not proof of absence, and brain-tissue MR was infeasible (no GTEx APOE eQTL at significance). Fifth, all primary GWAS are European-ancestry; trans-ethnic replication is future work. We explicitly evaluated whether the APOE direction could be independently verified in non-European cohorts; public summary statistics are access-restricted (IAMDGC 2.0 non-European AMD, dbGaP phs001039.v1.p1; MVP multi-ancestry AMD meta, dbGaP phs002453.v1.p1; ADGC multi-ancestry AD meta, n = 56,241, NIAGADS NG00177 qualified access; the 60,941-subject multi-ancestry LOAD study, GWAS Catalog GCST90654656, reports no full summary statistics; ADSP, dbGaP phs000572), so an independent trans-ethnic meta-analysis is not currently feasible. Convergent (non-independent) evidence exists on the AD side: the multi-ancestry ADGC meta-analysis (37,382 NHW, 6,728 African American, 8,899 Hispanic, 3,232 East Asian; Rajabli et al., Genome Biology 2025 [37]) reports the APOE locus among the genome-wide-significant cross-ancestry associations, with ε4 acting in the same risk direction in all four ancestries (effect sizes vary across populations); no public non-European AMD summary statistics are available for an AMD-side counterpart. Sixth, the mediation decomposition (product of coefficients) assumes a single mediator (APOE protein abundance) and a linear–log-odds scale; the direct-effect estimate varies by total-effect source (non-significant on the discovery platform, FinnGen R13; significant on the IAMDGC first-validation AMD cohort), so the proportion mediated is best read as a range, and the scale-sensitive product should not be over-interpreted as a point estimate. Seventh, strict pharmacovigilance did not support drug-specific repurposing; drug targets remain candidates requiring clinical validation. Eighth, no common-factor model is reported because two traits cannot identify one (df = −1; see Methods); the genetic relationship between AMD and AD is therefore summarised by the LDSC genetic correlation alone, which is underpowered (SE = 0.091) and cannot exclude |rg| up to ~0.2. Ninth, the ε2 mirror (Supplementary Table S20) is reported at the per-allele (additive) level, symmetric with the ε4 dose–response, and is genome-wide significant in the primary and independent AMD cohorts (AD protection β = −0.523, P = 2.4 × 10⁻⁷¹; AMD risk +0.146, P = 9.0 × 10⁻⁸ in FinnGen R13 and +0.155, P = 1.4 × 10⁻⁶ in IAMDGC late). It is nevertheless a per-allele (Wald) comparison rather than an instrument-based MR estimate, and the ε2-tag effect on APOE abundance differs in sign across proteomic sources under the second, non-UKB assay (UKB-PPP β = +0.745 versus second-source β = −0.207, likely a reference-allele/encoding difference under review), so we read the ε2 row as strong, directionally concordant mirror-allele corroboration of the level model rather than as an independent instrument-level bidirectional estimate. Tenth, the per-SNP deCODE analysis (Supplementary Table S15a,b) is **not an independent platform**: it re-analyses the same deCODE assay as the exposure-side replication (Supplementary Table S12), so it provides instrument-selection robustness within one exposure source rather than cross-platform replication; no numerical quantities are compared across the UKB-PPP and deCODE scales. Eleventh, the APOE-abundance model (Figure 4A,B) is a model forecast rather than a causal estimate: the linear form is supported at the heterozygous anchors but is not validated at two copies (the additivity audit gives a pooled observed/predicted ratio of 1.13, 95% CI 0.98–1.31, P = 0.10, with heterogeneity Q = 16.7, P = 0.054 and a deviation direction that reverses across onset strata), so quantitative extrapolation to drug-level abundance changes is not supported by these data and no APOE-directed therapy has been tested with AMD or AD as an endpoint. Likewise, the per-protein colocalization PP3 values are lead-variant/LD evidence derived from a thresholded pQTL file rather than full-region colocalization, and the pathway enrichment is coverage-limited (only 16 of the 69 KEGG complement-and-coagulation genes have GSMR estimates and no pathway survives correction across the KEGG sets that pass the size filter (15 sets with at least 10 measured proteins)). Twelfth, the single-cell and ligand–receptor analyses are descriptive: the co-expression programme, the receptor axis and the CellChat communication probabilities are correlational and dataset-specific, the CellChat analysis reports communication probabilities for the single database-annotated APOE pair rather than a genome-wide screen, and the complement programme is co-regulated with APOE level rather than caused by it; the network-level perturbation test that probes dependence on APOE itself is underpowered and parameter-unstable, and it failed external validation: only 1–4 genes per cell type reached FDR significance, the statistic uses distances in the aligned manifold rather than signed effects so it cannot assign a direction, and the network was restricted to 1,000 genes with three reconstructed networks per cell type because network construction scales with the square of the gene count, so the absence of a strong signal is not evidence of absence. In addition, the predictions showed no target-specific concordance with the only accessible human APOE-knockdown transcriptome (human astrocytes; a single control sample; coverage-based quantification), which may reflect the cell-type mismatch as much as a failure of the method.

In summary, convergent multi-omic evidence indicates that APOE protein levels carry opposite causal effects on AMD and Alzheimer's disease, that this effect is systemic (plasma-mediated) and APOE-specific, providing a mechanistic framework for the ε4 paradox as a protein-abundance-driven bidirectional causal switch between the two leading degenerative diseases of old age. APOE abundance is thereby a systemic state that protects the brain while endangering the retina — a disease-level protein antagonism with a testable cross-disease safety prediction (APOE-lowering forecasts lower AMD risk, not higher). The signature of this model is not a shared genetic architecture but a **locus-restricted** one: the two diseases show no detectable genome-wide genetic correlation (rg = −0.026, SE 0.091, P = 0.77), yet the APOE locus itself carries a strong negative local genetic correlation (LAVA rho = −0.65, 95% CI −0.74 to −0.57, P = 3 × 10⁻⁴¹) — the pattern expected if a single shared molecular state, rather than shared genetic aetiology, drives the two risks in opposite directions. Upstream APOE-directed development and downstream inflammatory targets remain candidate therapeutic directions requiring clinical validation, with a testable cross-disease safety prediction for APOE-lowering therapies.

## Methods

**Data sources.** Primary outcomes: FinnGen R13 (sumstats via finngen.fi; H7_AMD N = 472,750; G6_ALZHEIMER N = 500,186). First validation: IAMDGC late AMD (N = 32,339; 16,144 late AMD cases)[6] for AMD and Jansen 2019[38] (European; N = 455,258 = 71,880 clinical and proxy cases + 383,378 controls; per-SNP N in the released file varies and reaches ≈ 430,000; contains the APOE region; no-UKB status pending pre-submission human verification) for AD. Second validation: the Million Veteran Program (AMD; PheCode 362_2, multi-ancestry) and Bellenguez 2022[22] (AD; European ancestry; contains the FinnGen component; APOE ε4/ε2 coding variants absent from the released summary statistics). The published case–control counts of that meta-analysis (111,326 cases + 677,663 controls) are not recoverable from the released summary statistics: the GCST90027158 file reports per-variant n = n_cases + n_controls, whose maximum is 487,511 (85,934 cases + 401,577 controls), and 12 classical APOE-region tag variants are absent from it altogether. This cohort therefore enters only as a directional validation, and no case-fraction-dependent quantity is derived from it. Dose–response pooling and FinnGen-independent direction check: Kunkle 2019[23] (N = 94,437 = 35,274 cases + 59,163 controls; a component of Bellenguez). UK Biobank AMD (N ≈ 30,000, ICD-coded, underpowered) is reported as a sensitivity comparison. FinnGen R8 is not used (it shares participants with the discovery cohort). pQTL from UKB-PPP[21] (2,923 proteins; pQTL analysis N ≈ 34,000). GTEx v11. 1000 Genomes European (EU1000QC) LD reference. Retinal eQTL: EyeGEx (n = 129) and Strunz 2020 mega-analysis (n = 311). Single-cell: GSE230348 (75 samples, four AMD stages; 92,439 scored cells used for cell-type frameworks).

**AMD subtype analysis.** FinnGen R13 subtype endpoints DRY_AMD (dry AMD, including geographic atrophy; 9,533 cases / 333,701 controls) and WET_AMD (wet/neovascular AMD; 7,529 cases / 335,608 controls) were analysed with the same 11 clumped UKB-PPP APOE cis-pQTL instruments, harmonization and estimators as the primary analysis; the all-AMD endpoint (H7_AMD) was re-run through the identical pipeline as a positive control and reproduced the primary estimate exactly (IVW β = +0.181777146570588, P = 7.89243 × 10⁻¹¹). The effect allele in the FinnGen R13 files is the alternative allele (rs429358-C = ε4; rs7412-T = ε2), so the reported per-allele effects are per-copy ε4 and ε2. Case and control counts are from the FinnGen R13 endpoint table (r13.finngen.fi). The two subtype endpoints overlap — their case counts sum to more than H7_AMD — so they are not a partition of the all-AMD phenotype and subtype-specific estimates are endpoint-level contrasts. MVP subtype estimates (PheCode 362_21 dry, 362_22 wet, trans-ancestry meta-analysis) are from the same MVP analysis as the per-ancestry estimates.

**GSMR.** GCTA-GSMR[24], cis instruments at P < 5 × 10⁻⁸ (clumped at r² = 0.001, 10 Mb window), HEIDI filtering applied internally (gsmr v1.0.6); 2,923 protein targets were assessed, 679 with a GSMR estimate (at least two cis-pQTL instruments after allele harmonization) per analysis; multiple testing: Bonferroni across all 2,923 and BH-FDR across the 679 tested. The same pipeline and denominators were applied to the two new outcome cohorts (IAMDGC late AMD; Jansen 2019 AD), giving 13 and 5 Bonferroni-significant proteins respectively (Supplementary Table S1b).

**Instrument variable MR.** MendelianRandomization R package v0.10.0 (IVW[39], MR-Egger[40], weighted median[41], MR-PRESSO[42], leave-one-out; `mr_ivw_mre` not used for primary results). Primary analysis: inverse-variance weighted (IVW)[39] with the multiplicative overdispersion correction that is the default of `MendelianRandomization::mr_ivw` — the fixed-effect standard error is multiplied by max(1, √(Q/(k−1))), where Q is Cochran's heterogeneity statistic and k the number of instruments, and this correction is applied **unconditionally** (also when Q is not significant, e.g. FinnGen R13 AMD Q = 11.0, df = 10, P = 0.36). This is **not** a DerSimonian–Laird random-effects IVW; the two differ materially (DL random effects: dry AMD β = 0.167, wet AMD β = 0.204). Fixed-effect IVW would give the same point estimates with narrower intervals (FinnGen R13 all AMD, P = 9.1 × 10⁻¹² instead of 7.9 × 10⁻¹¹; dry AMD, P = 2.7 × 10⁻¹⁰ instead of 3.9 × 10⁻⁶; wet AMD, P = 4.0 × 10⁻⁶ instead of 1.3 × 10⁻³); the overdispersion-corrected values reported throughout are the more conservative of the two. Instruments harmonized for allele direction. Directionality was assessed with Steiger filtering[43] (TwoSampleMR implementation). Wald ratios computed as β_GWAS / β_pQTL with delta-method SEs. **Per-allele (additive) model.** All per-allele APOE effects in this study (ε4 tag rs429358; ε2 tag rs7412) are the additive per-copy GWAS effects of these tag SNPs. Summary statistics provide per-allele effects only, so genotype-level effects (e.g., ε2/ε3 vs ε3/ε3) are not estimable from our data; the published ε2/ε3 OR in Supplementary Table S20[3] derives from individual-level case-control genotyping (Farrer 1997) and is used as an external anchor, not as an estimate from our data. For the ε4 dose–response pooling, a cross-cohort scale-consistency gate was applied: a cohort enters the inverse-variance quantitative pool only if its per-allele ε4 effect is within ±50% of the anchor scale (Kunkle 2019, β = +1.202; FinnGen R13, β = +0.995). Jansen 2019 (β = +0.162; scale ratio 0.125) failed the gate and is reported as directional/significance validation only (same discipline as the Kunkle 2019 sign-anomaly handling). APOE-family panel: 20 proteins × {AMD, AD}, clumped UKB-PPP instruments (r² = 0.001, 10 Mb), xargs-parallel (20 workers); APOE row validated against the independent focus-MR pipeline to four decimal places before any protein result was accepted.

**APOE-abundance scale.** UKB-PPP pQTL effect sizes are used as reported[21]. The percentage changes quoted for the ε4 and ε2 alleles (≈−64% and +110%) assume a natural-log scale (e^β); in the units of the source summary statistics the per-allele changes are −1.012 (ε4) and +0.745 (ε2). If the source betas are instead on an inverse-normal-transformed (per-SD) scale, the corresponding changes are −1.01 SD and +0.75 SD. Direction and significance of every MR estimate are unaffected by this choice, but the absolute percentage should be read with this caveat.

**Clumping robustness grid.** Full 1,407-variant APOE pQTL set re-clumped at r² ∈ {0.0001, 0.001, 0.01} × window ∈ {5, 10} Mb using pairwise r² from EU1000QC (1,239/1,407 variants present; greedy p-value-ordered clump; LD from 503 HapMap genotypes). Each grid cell: IVW/Egger/WMed (Wald when <3 instruments). All runs deterministic; parallel MR cells byte-identical on re-run (verified).

**Tissue (retinal) MR.** EyeGEx APOE pairs (4 variants; best P = 5.3 × 10⁻⁸; F ≈ 27) and Strunz 2020 top-10 (best P = 1.5 × 10⁻⁴; F ≈ 13; reported as weak-instrument sensitivity). Wald(best), IVW, MR-Egger, weighted median, mean F, and R²-based Steiger (exposure = retinal eQTL, n = 129/311; outcome = disease GWAS). Throughout, the Steiger R² of a multi-variant instrument set is the **sum of the per-variant R²** (Σ r_i², the convention of `TwoSampleMR::mr_steiger`, whose source computes `sqrt(sum(r_exp^2))`), not their mean; the reported z statistics are on that scale, and P values beyond the double-precision limit (z ≳ 38) are given as P < 10⁻³⁰⁰.

**Colocalization.** Two tiers. (i) Classic single-signal: coloc R package[44], coloc.abf, default priors (p1 = p2 = 1 × 10⁻⁴, p12 = 1 × 10⁻⁵), with the full 1,407-variant APOE pQTL set (unclumped) and the 11-instrument clumped level set (ε4 coding tag excluded by design). The released p column is truncated at 0 for the strongest APOE-region variants (31 such variants in the FinnGen R13 AD file, including rs429358; 30 of them survive the LD-reference filter applied here). Those variants carry |z| = 41.8–73.1, and a p value recomputed from β/SE underflows to zero as well in double precision (|z| ≳ 37.5), so recomputing p does not recover them. The analyses above therefore do not filter on p at all: variants are retained on finite β and positive SE. This matters because a `p > 0` filter removes precisely the strongest signals in the region. In this configuration coloc.abf derives its approximate Bayes factors from β and varbeta (both supplied), so the p column does not enter the likelihood in any case. Case fractions (s) from cohort case counts: AMD FinnGen R13 s = 13,947/472,750 (case count recovered from the allele-frequency fields of the released FinnGen R13 file; PP4 is stable across a 5.5k–16k/N band), AMD IAMDGC s = 16,144/32,339 (Fritsche 2016), AD FinnGen R13 s = 16,652/500,186, AD Jansen s = 71,880/455,258 (clinical + proxy). (ii) Fine-mapping: see below. A multi-signal (suSiE-based) colocalization was attempted and is deliberately not reported: in this region every credible set collapsed to a single variant, the LD matrix was not positive semidefinite, and the agreement between the summary statistics and the 1000 Genomes European LD reference was low (s_rss = 0.68 for the pQTL and 0.71 for the AD GWAS), so the analysis would report LD structure rather than shared or distinct causal variants.

**Genetic correlation.** Two complementary approaches. (i) GenomicSEM multivariate LDSC (GenomicSEM 0.0.5[25]) on FinnGen R13 AMD/AD sumstats (200 LD blocks, EU1000QC reference): estimated the bivariate genetic architecture (h²_AMD, h²_AD, rg) on the observed scale. (ii) Standard LDSC (ldsc.py maintained build) --rg on munged AMD/AD sumstats with 1000G EUR LD scores (eur_w_ld_chr): cross-method validation. **Regional local genetic correlation** was estimated with the official LAVA implementation (LAVA 0.1.5; `process.input` / `process.locus` / `run.bivar`) on the APOE region (chr19:44–46 Mb, hg19) using the EU1000QC reference panel (503 individuals) with FinnGen R13 AMD and AD; both traits come from the same cohort, so no sample-overlap matrix could be supplied and the analysis assumes none — because a positive phenotypic correlation between the two diseases would bias the estimate towards zero, the negative estimate reported here is conservative. The point estimate is deterministic across repeated runs; the confidence interval and the simulation-based P value vary by about 1–2% between runs. Estimates whose interval touches ±1 were not interpretable (the local genetic covariance is then unidentified) and are not reported; three equally sized non-APOE windows on chromosome 19 served as a calibration set, and an additional 29 tiled 2-Mb windows of chromosome 19 (19 estimable) provided an empirical distribution (`revision/118_lava_scan.R`, `revision/119_lava_chr19_windows.R`).

**Per-SNP analysis within the deCODE exposure source.** The deCODE APOE pQTL was additionally analysed at per-SNP resolution (the per-SNP source file contains 30.85M genome-wide rows; N = 35,367–35,372 per SNP), and the same 11-instrument set adopted for the clumped primary analysis (r² = 0.001, 10 Mb) was re-scored on this source, with MR re-run on FinnGen R13 AMD (H7), FinnGen R13 AD (G6) and IAMDGC late AMD (IVW / MR-Egger / weighted median; Supplementary Table S15a,b). This is the same deCODE assay used in the exposure-side replication above, not an independent platform: the per-SNP tag estimates are numerically identical between the two analyses (revision/results/r13/crosssource_concordance.csv). Because IVW is invariant to scale (not rotation) of the exposure axis, only direction and significance are compared across the two exposure sources (UKB-PPP versus deCODE); the two scales are not directly comparable (e.g., ε4 tag β = −0.2228 in deCODE versus −1.012 in UKB-PPP). Three single-SNP tag checks (rs429358, rs71352239, rs7412) were added as directional probes.

**Exposure-side replication with deCODE (Icelandic Olink).** APOE cis-pQTL instruments were clumped from the deCODE Olink proteomics cohort (N = 35,367–35,372 per SNP; Icelandic origin, no UK Biobank overlap with the FinnGen R13 AMD/AD outcome cohorts). Six clumped SNPs were used (r² threshold 0.0001, 5 Mb window) for the primary IVW/MR-Egger/weighted median runs; three single-SNP tag checks (rs429358, rs71352239, plus the French/UK-side best APOE-effect SNP) were added as directional consistency probes. Estimated on FinnGen R13 AMD (H7), FinnGen R13 AD (G6), and IAMDGC late AMD; the IVW β + P values are reported alongside the primary UKB-PPP GSMR (numbers in Supplementary Table S12).

**Fine-mapping.** SuSiE[45] (susieR v0.14.2) on the APOE region (chr19:44.9–45.8 Mb) using EU1000QC LD, z-scores = β/SE, L = 10, coverage 0.95, scaled prior variance 0.01. For Jansen 2019, convergence was attempted in two stages (full-region top 1,000 by p < 10⁻³ with extended IBSS iterations; local APOE-gene window 45.30–45.50 Mb with |z| > 1.96); IBSS did not converge in either (100-iteration ceiling; maximal z ≈ 52 exceeds the scaled prior support — the same behaviour as Kunkle 2019). Jansen is therefore reported descriptively (per-variant z table, Supplementary Table S1c) as sensitivity, not as posterior PIPs.

**Mediation analysis (product of coefficients).** The per-allele effect of the ε4 tag (rs429358) on each disease was decomposed into an indirect component (ε4 → APOE → disease) and a direct residual. Branch a: ε4 → APOE, the UKB-PPP unclumped pQTL estimate for rs429358 (β = −1.012, SE = 0.010). Branch b: APOE → disease, the focal clumped IVW estimate (11 instruments, r² = 0.001, 10 Mb, ε4 coding tag excluded by design; AMD β = +0.182, SE = 0.028; AD β = −0.942, SE = 0.109). Total effect: per-allele rs429358 GWAS estimate on FinnGen R13 (AMD: −0.204, SE = 0.018; AD: +0.995, SE = 0.014). Indirect = a × b with delta-method SE = √(b²·SE_a² + a²·SE_b²); direct = total − indirect with SE = √(SE_total² + SE_indirect²) (conservative independence assumption). The identical decomposition was computed for the ε2 tag (rs7412; a = +0.745, SE = 0.012; totals: AMD +0.146, SE = 0.027; AD −0.523, SE = 0.029); for ε2–AD the indirect path exceeds the total, so no proportion is interpreted. Because both branches quantify the same UKB-PPP APOE quantity, the product is dimensionally consistent with the total per-allele effect; the absolute proportion is scale-sensitive (protein-scale coding), so the primary claim is the non-significance of the direct pathway. The decomposition on the first-validation AMD cohort (IAMDGC total effect) is retained as a sensitivity analysis (56.1% mediated; direct path significant, P = 6.6 × 10⁻⁴). Script: revision/65_mediation_r13.R; output: apoe_results/mediation_results_r13.csv (deterministic; R 4.5.3, data.table 1.18.4).

**ScPagwas (reported for completeness).** ScPagwas 2.0.0[46] was applied to the same matrix (92,439 cells × 309 KEGG pathways, 200 pathway-bootstrap iterations per disease); no cell type survived FDR correction in either disease and the gene-level ranking was not robust to the pathway-set definition, so the analysis is not interpreted and its results and figures are withdrawn.

**Single-cell frameworks.** scDRS (v1.0.2) on GSE230348 scored cells: gene sets = MAGMA[47] genes with P < 5 × 10⁻⁸ (AMD: FinnGen; AD: Bellenguez), weights = MAGMA Z; n_ctrl = 200 permutations; cell-type contrasts = Mann–Whitney per cell type vs rest, Benjamini–Hochberg FDR (10 tests). seismicGWAS (v1.0.0, Lai 2025): calc_specificity (min_ct_size = 50) on log1p-transformed expression; cell-type–trait associations via MAGMA Z (GENE/ZSTAT); driver genes via find_inf_genes.

**Stage-stratified single-cell analysis.** GSE230348 pseudobulk per sample; APOE expression regressed on stage order (OLS), overall and per tissue.

**Mechanism analyses (2026-09-09; extended 2026-09-09 v53).** *Per-protein colocalization*: for each disease-associated protein we ran coloc.abf (coloc 5.2.3, the same priors as the APOE analysis) between the protein's cis-pQTL set (local UKB-PPP file, P < 5 x 10^-8-thresholded) and the FinnGen R13 AMD and AD signals, and recorded PP3/PP4, the lead pQTL and disease variants, and the LD (r2, EU1000QC) between them; CFD was excluded because its cis variants do not overlap the disease files. *Retinal-eQTL SMR*: SMR[48] (v1.3.1) with retinal cis-eQTL instruments (EyeGEx, n = 129; Strunz 2020, n = 311) against FinnGen R13 AMD, IAMDGC late AMD and FinnGen R13 AD, using the EU1000QC LD reference; the HEIDI test was attempted but is unavailable for APOE because only one cis-eQTL instrument survived pruning. *Cross-disease protein network*: for every protein with a GSMR estimate in both diseases we recorded direction, raw and BH-adjusted P and HEIDI status, counted opposite-signed proteins, and identified proteins significant in both after BH correction. *APOE-abundance model forecast and additivity audit*: log OR = beta_MR x Delta-log(APOE), with beta_MR the clumped-instrument IVW estimate in FinnGen R13 (AMD +0.182; AD -0.942) and 95% CIs propagated from its SE; anchors were the per-allele epsilon-4 and epsilon-2 effects (Delta-log APOE = -1.012 and +0.745; per-allele disease effects from the same cohort) and published genotype-stratified homozygote odds ratios (Farrer 1997[3]; Genin 2011[27]; McKay 2011[5]), positioned at twice the per-allele abundance change. The additivity audit compares the observed homozygote OR with the additive prediction OR_het^2 in each published stratum, with delta-method 95% CIs and a fixed-effect inverse-variance pooled estimate plus a Q test for heterogeneity. This is a model forecast, not a causal estimate. *Pathway enrichment*: fgsea (v1.36.2) on the GSMR z-scores (bxy/bxy_se) of the 675 proteins with estimates, against MSigDB C2:CP:KEGG 2023.1 (186 sets) and hallmark 2023.1 (50 sets); a sensitivity analysis used the curated KEGG complement list with a signed -log10 P ranking, and Fisher exact tests compared significant proteins against the 675-protein universe. *APOE receptor axis*: per-gene, per-cell-type percentage of expressing cells and mean log-normalised expression for 15 APOE receptor/lipid-transport genes across the six GSE230348 cell types (`revision/101_apoe_receptor_axis.py`; `revision/results/mechanism/apoe_receptor_axis.csv`). *APOE co-expression programme*: within each cell type, the partial correlation of APOE with every detected gene, controlling for log total UMI and log detected genes, on log-normalised (CP10K, log1p) expression, computed with the official `ppcor::pcor.test` function (`revision/116b_coexpr_ppcor_official.R`; `revision/results/mechanism/apoe_coexpression_official_*.csv`); genes with zero expression in a cell type have an undefined partial correlation and are excluded (this is exactly the set on which `pcor.test` fails, verified per cell type). Pathway enrichment of the co-expression ranking with fgsea against the KEGG collection (`revision/103b_coexpr_pathway_ppcor.R`; `revision/results/mechanism/apoe_coexpression_pathways_ppcor_allgenes.csv`). A previous version of this analysis used an in-house partial-correlation implementation that omitted the 1/n scaling of the cross-product matrix; the discrepancy and its correction are documented in `docs/audit_20260909/PPCOR_OFFICIAL_RECOMPUTE_20260910.md`, and all co-expression numbers reported here are those of the official implementation. *CellChat*: CellChat 2.2.0.9001 with CellChatDB.human on the GSE230348 expression matrix, triMean communication probabilities and min.cells = 10; the only database-annotated APOE ligand–receptor pair is APOE → TREM2–TYROBP (ApoE pathway), and communication strengths are reported per source → target pair (`revision/105_cellchat_apoe.R`; `revision/results/mechanism/cellchat_APOE_TREM2_TYROBP.csv`). *scDRS circularity check*: the scDRS scoring was repeated with all 58 genes in the APOE region (chr19:44–46 Mb) removed from both gene sets (`revision/106_scdrs_circularity.py`; `revision/results/scdrs_apoe/scdrs_celltype_tests_noAPOEregion.csv`). *Region-versus-genome-wide effect correlation*: Pearson correlation of AMD and AD per-variant effect sizes (z) across LD-pruned (r² < 0.2) APOE-region variants (chr19:44–46 Mb) and genome-wide HapMap3 variants, with a two-sided binomial test on the proportion of opposite-sign variants (`revision/104_region_rg_pruned.py`; `revision/results/mechanism/region_vs_genome_effect_correlation.csv`). *Virtual knockout*: scTenifoldPerturb 0.1.3 (weigenwu/scTenifoldPerturb) on the scTenifoldNet 1.4 engine, which zeroes the target gene's outgoing edges in the reconstructed wild-type network (perturb factor 0) (`revision/111_sctenifold_perturb_APOE.R`). Only the knockout direction is reported: the official scTenifold family (scTenifoldNet / scTenifoldKnk / scTenifoldXct) provides no overexpression mode, so no official implementation exists for it. A rerun with the official scTenifoldKnk package at its default parameters is in progress; the numbers reported here are those of the scTenifoldPerturb knockout run. Per cell type the network was reconstructed from raw counts restricted to 1,000 genes (95 force-included APOE-family, complement, receptor-axis, AD-risk and retinal genes plus the 905 most variable genes; three networks of 300 subsampled cells each; five principal components; CP rank 3; manifold dimension 3), and differential regulation was tested with the package's Box-Cox/chi-square procedure and BH correction. The perturbation was verified to act as specified: the APOE out-edge weight sum changed from the wild-type value to exactly zero under knockout in every cell type. The statistic is a distance in the aligned manifold rather than a signed effect, so it tests dependence on APOE's outgoing edges and cannot assign a direction. A higher-depth rerun in RPE, endothelium and microglia used 10 networks of 500 subsampled cells and 700 genes. Results of both runs are in Supplementary Table S32. *External validation of the perturbation*: the predicted rankings were tested against GSE263862 (human astrocytes; five siAPOE versus one siAPOE non-targeting control, and three siAANCR versus one siAANCR control as an unrelated-target negative control). Gene-level expression was obtained by summing hg38 bigWig coverage over UCSC refGene gene bodies, converting to counts per million, and taking the log2 ratio between the knockdown and control groups; concordance with each predicted ranking was measured by Spearman correlation against |log2 fold change|, by the AUROC for classifying the top decile of the true response, and by Fisher enrichment of the predicted top 50 among the true top 500, with the criterion (AUROC ≥ 0.60 and Fisher P < 0.05 and outperforming the negative control) fixed before the test (`revision/113_gse263862_apoe_kd_signature.py`, `revision/114_perturbation_concordance.py`).

**Drug target prioritization.** Cross-disease proteome comparison prioritized targets with opposite or disease-specific causal effects and existing approved or late-stage drugs.

**Pharmacovigilance.** FAERS (2020 Q1–2026 Q1) and CVARD (1965–2026); strict AD/AMD MedDRA PTs; ROR with 95% CI, suspect-role, age ≥ 50 primary stratum, negative-control drugs.

**Statistical analysis.** Two core estimators: (i) GSMR (gsmr 1.0.6 pipeline; coloc 5.2.3 as HEIDI + coloc backend), reporting IVW β (log-odds per unit APOE), SE, P, 95% CI = β ± 1.96 · SE; (ii) clumped-instrument TwoSampleMR IVW (v0.7.9)[49] with the same β/SE/CI. 95% CIs on log-odds are OR-converted by e^β (bounds e^(β ± 1.96 · SE)). General MR reporting follows the 2023 STROBE-MR-aligned guidelines[50]. Multiple testing for the proteome-wide scan: Bonferroni across all 2,923 assayed proteins (conservative; main text reports these hits) and Benjamini–Hochberg FDR across the proteins with a GSMR estimate (at least two cis-pQTL instruments) per analysis — 679 in the discovery cohort, 693 in IAMDGC late AMD and 746 in Jansen 2019 AD (Supplementary Table S1b; 13 and 5 Bonferroni hits respectively); exploratory families (clumping grid k=10, deCODE exposure replication k=18, second-assay exposure-source replication k=12) are reported without multiplicity correction, family-level P ranges in S7/S12/S15. Each MR stage was re-validated against the golden anchor (see Software and versions). Mediation (product of coefficients): delta-method SEs as in Methods; single pre-specified decomposition, no multiplicity family.
